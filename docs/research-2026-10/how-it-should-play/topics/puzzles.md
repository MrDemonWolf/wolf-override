# Puzzles, interactions and readability — research notes (WOLF//OVERRIDE)

Researcher topic: puzzles, interactions and readability. Started 2026-10-12. Notes are written as I go; sources listed at the end with ids [PUZZLES-n].

Game context checked in code (2026-10-12): corridor MANUAL BYPASS = 8 held ticks x 0.4 s = 3.2 s hold (bypass_handle.gd TICKS_NEEDED 8, hold_use.gd TICK_SECONDS 0.4); engineer reach 17 px, WOLF reach 67 px (m0_actor.gd); Service Junction interactables at x 200 breaker, 300 vent, 420 valve, 480 door, arm panel, 806 bolt panel, 880 hatch (junction_room.gd) — about 6-7 USE points across ~900 px of room; prompts are text in a bottom ContextHint bar ("HOLD E: crank ...").

## Findings (in progress)

### F1. Signifiers, not affordances [PUZZLES-1]
Norman (ACM Interactions 15(6), 2008): an affordance is Gibson's "relationship: the actions possible by a specific agent on a specific environment"; a signifier is "some sort of indicator, some signal in the physical or social world that can be interpreted meaningfully". Conclusion: "Forget affordances: what people need, and what design must provide, are signifiers." Signifiers can be accidental as well as deliberate, and can signify by absence.
WOLF//OVERRIDE: a wheel that looks crankable is the affordance; what the player needs is a signifier that THIS wheel is live now (lamp state, hiss, WOLF looking at it, a worn grip). Accidental signifiers cut both ways: a painted wheel in the background that is not usable is a false signifier ("weird doors", "hovering items" read as signifiers of something). Rule: anything that looks operable at gameplay depth must either work or be visibly dead (capped, tagged out, no lamp).

### F2. Ron Gilbert, Why Adventure Games Suck (1989) [PUZZLES-2]
Grumpy Gamer repost dated 12 May 2004 ("I wrote this back in 1989 while I was designing Monkey Island"); first printed in the December 1989 issue of The Journal of Computer Game Design (per Jimmy Maher, The Digital Antiquarian, 2017 [PUZZLES-43]; the Grumpy Gamer page itself does not name the journal). 12 rules: clear end objective; obvious sub-goals; live and learn; backwards puzzles; I forgot to pick it up; puzzles should advance the story; real time is bad drama; incremental reward; arbitrary puzzles; reward intent; unconnected events; give the player options. Quotes: "It is bad design to put puzzles and situations into a game that require a player to die in order to learn what not to do next time." "If the solution can only be reached by trial and error or plain luck, it's a bad puzzle." "Ideally, the crevice should be found before the rope that allows the player to descend."
WOLF//OVERRIDE: show the problem (sealed door, overloaded line, sentry lane) before the tool; never require a first death to learn a hazard (Blowback sentry lane, tank drop); "reward intent": if the engineer is at the valve with the right goal, a near-miss on position should still work (current HUMAN_REACH 17 px is tight — see F-later). "Real time is bad drama": the alarm during the bypass crank is pressure, not a timer that fails you — consistent with "time, never progress".

### F3. Blow and ten Bosch, IndieCade 2011 [PUZZLES-3]
Report by Mathew Kumar, Game Developer, 8 Oct 2011. Blow: surprise is "the information content of a message"; in The Witness "when you came upon a puzzle, you knew instantly that it was a puzzle". Puzzles come from exploring a rule's implications ("that's something that came from exploration, not from a top-down decision").
WOLF//OVERRIDE: a lush, real-looking base can coexist with instant puzzle recognition if the puzzle objects share one visual language (same lamp, same stencil family, same "live" light) distinct from set dressing.

### F4. Mikael Kasurinen (Remedy), against excessive yellow paint [PUZZLES-4]
Game Developer, Bryant Francis, 29 Sep 2026. "If it feels like it's like 'go here,' it's not anymore a decision for the player." Alternative: consistent environmental rules (fixed gravity points shaping rain and wind) that players learn to read. Fact-check note: the article is about navigation and wayfinding in Control Resonant, not interactable objects, so applying it to interactables is our extension.
WOLF//OVERRIDE: use consistent diegetic rules (live equipment = lit lamp + sound; dead equipment = dark, tagged) rather than paint; but on phone screens the cue must still survive small size and darkness.

### F5. Naughty Dog, The Last of Us Part II accessibility (2020) [PUZZLES-5]
Matthew Gallant (Lead Systems Designer, Naughty Dog), PlayStation Blog, 9 Jun 2020: 60+ settings. Skip Puzzle: "select SKIP PUZZLE from the pause menu to advance to the puzzle's completion." High Contrast Display "adds distinct contrast coloring to allies, enemies, items, and interactive objects." Traversal audio cues for "jumpable gaps, climbable ledges, squeeze-throughs". "we have individual options to change every button hold into a toggle, and every rapid press into a hold".
WOLF//OVERRIDE: the 3.2 s crank hold needs a hold-to-toggle option; an optional high-contrast interactable mode; a skip-puzzle option for the Blowback beat is cheap because every puzzle already has a deterministic completed state.

### F6. God of War Ragnarök companion hints too early [PUZZLES-6]
Matt Sophos (narrative director, Santa Monica Studio; story lead Richard Gaubert also on the call) on MinnMax, reported by Shahmeer Sarfaraz, eXputer, 17 Feb 2023: "The timing for the first hint was too aggressive and should have had a much longer countdown timer before something comes up." Sophos said the problem "didn't get exposed to us really until after the game came out". The eXputer piece mentions no patch. The "Reduced Puzzle Hints" option shipped first in the PC version (PlayStation Blog, 18 Sep 2024 [PUZZLES-24]) and reached consoles in Update 6.00 on 7 Nov 2024 (MP1st, Alex Co, citing Santa Monica Support [PUZZLES-44]). (Primary video not opened; quote via eXputer: SECONDARY.)
WOLF//OVERRIDE: WOLF is a talking companion who reads gauges; his hints must start late and escalate, and should be optional/turn-down-able. Biggest companion-puzzle pitfall in the corpus.

### F7. Jeppe Carlsen (Cocoon, ex-Limbo/Inside lead gameplay designer) [PUZZLES-7][PUZZLES-8]
Remap Radio, Patrick Klepek, 7 Nov 2023 (email interview): "trust is very important when it comes to puzzle games"; "Each wrong attempt at solving a puzzle needs to very clearly give the feedback that the solution did not work, that it never will work (with repeated attempts), and why it won't work." "The more complex the thing I am making is, the more simple and accessible it also needs to be". Premortem Games, Eric Bartelson, 18 Sep 2024: "The one thing I never compromise on is playability. The art, sounds, and animation need to communicate the puzzle logic effectively."
WOLF//OVERRIDE: every wrong action needs a distinct "no, and here's why" response (e.g., valve cranked before breaker armed: wheel turns but LINE gauge stays at zero and WOLF glances at the breaker; tank dropped early: arm clanks on an empty mark and the sentry startles but no death). Art polish must never hide puzzle logic — the "no AI slop" rule is a readability rule too.

### F8. Mark Brown, What Makes a Good Puzzle? (GMTK, 14 Mar 2018) [PUZZLES-9]
Read from the video's English subtitles (yt-dlp, not stored in repo). Six parts: mechanics, the catch, the revelation, the assumption, presentation, the curve. Key lines: the goal must be clear — "The player shouldn't be figuring out what to do - just how to do it." The catch = "a logical contradiction, where two things are seemingly in direct conflict". Best solutions are "effortless to actually execute". Braid's clone-bounce puzzle "felt more like a trick than a revelation", made worse because "there's only one specific moment when it can happen, meaning players couldn't easily experiment." Presentation: "a good puzzle is pretty minimalist, with almost no extraneous elements"; Portal's lines from buttons to doors "change colour when powered up, to clearly explain how the room is put together"; Rise of the Tomb Raider puts the platform "significantly far away so it's clearly impossible to get there in time" so a wrong assumption reads as wrong, not as "almost". Square Enix Montreal (GO games) rate difficulty by four criteria: number of possible solutions, number of steps, "the number of options the player can choose from at each moment", and prior mechanics needed.
WOLF//OVERRIDE: (a) the Blowback's goal (get through the sealed door, then the exit bolt) must be stated up front; only the HOW is the puzzle. (b) Wire the junction like Portal: visible conduit from breaker -> coolant line -> valve -> door, lit segment by segment as each step arms (diegetic, real-looking cable trays, not paint). (c) The tank drop is a one-moment timing puzzle like Braid's: give a repeating window (the sentry crosses the mark "on every pass" — already in BAIT_NO_LINE) so players can experiment. (d) A wrong-but-plausible attempt must look clearly wrong, never "almost".

### F9. Brown, Puzzle Solving... or Problem Solving? (GMTK, 2015) [PUZZLES-10]
Game Maker's Toolkit, 18 Jun 2015 (YouTube upload date). Contrasts discovering "the" solution (Portal) with inventing "a" solution in engineering games (Infinifactory, SpaceChem); "by taking inspiration from real-world problems, like programming or engineering games like Spacechem and World of Goo can actually be more fun than those built from arbitrary puzzles." Fact-check nuance: Brown's argument is about OPEN-ENDED games with many valid solutions. WOLF//OVERRIDE's Junction is a single-solution chain, so this source supports "real-world framing reads better than arbitrary riddles" only loosely; it does not argue for linear maintenance puzzles.
WOLF//OVERRIDE: the engineer is a systems engineer in a real-looking machine base — puzzles should read as real maintenance problems (isolate, reroute, bleed pressure, lock out) rather than abstract riddles. That is also the anti-"moon logic" rule.

### F10. Hayashida / Miyamoto four-step (kishōtenketsu) [PUZZLES-11]
Game Developer (Gamasutra), Christian Nutt, 13 Apr 2012, interview with Koichi Hayashida (director of Super Mario 3D Land): "First, you have to learn how to use that gameplay mechanic, and then the stage will offer you a slightly more complicated scenario in which you have to use it. And then the next step is something crazy happens that makes you think about it in a way you weren't expecting. And then you get to demonstrate, finally, what sort of mastery you've gained over it."
WOLF//OVERRIDE: corridor = learn (breaker -> relay; crank = slow but safe), Records = develop (copy, second source), Junction = twist (overload the line ON PURPOSE to blow a door — the corridor taught overload as a fault to avoid), exit bolt = mastery (dump the overload into the bolt yourself). The current slice already has this shape; make the twist's reversal explicit in WOLF's line.

### F11. Ron Gilbert, Puzzle Dependency Charts (Grumpy Gamer, 10 Aug 2014) [PUZZLES-12]
"Solving one puzzle should open up 2 or 3 new ones, and then those collapses down ... to a single solution". Design backwards from goals: "I usually start with 'The player needs to get into the basement'".
WOLF//OVERRIDE: chart the Junction: goal = exit hatch <- bolt powered <- tank winch power freed <- sentry down <- door blown <- valve cranked <- line overloaded <- breaker armed. It is a strict chain of 7-8 nodes with no parallelism; that is fine for a 1-3 minute action beat but means any unclear node blocks everything — each node needs its own signifier and feedback.

### F12. Return to Monkey Island hint book [PUZZLES-13]
Inverse, Joseph Yaden, 6 Sep 2022 (PAX West interview with Ron Gilbert and Dave Grossman): "Unlike looking stuff over the internet, the hint book actually knows where you are in the game." "We know what puzzles you have solved. And we can really tailor all the questions we ask you".
WOLF//OVERRIDE: WOLF's hint lines can be state-aware cheaply because the game state is deterministic (breaker armed? valve ticks? sentry state?): pick the hint for the FIRST unmet node in the F11 chain, never a generic one.

### F13. Teikari, Baba Is You [PUZZLES-14][PUZZLES-15]
Game Developer Road to the IGF, Joel Couture, 15 Feb 2018: "the most satisfying moments in puzzle games are those which present the player with simple but hard-to-wrap-your-head-around situations"; "Playtesting has shown that it's extremely easy to be blind to certain types of unintended solutions". MCV/Develop, Marie Dealessandri, 8 Aug 2019: "If you have a single developer making all the design decisions it's very easy to become blind to the difficulty." Variant levels came about "because a tester found an alternative solution."
WOLF//OVERRIDE: one designer (plus AI agents) is exactly the blind-to-difficulty risk; fresh-player tests are the only real check. Alternative solutions in an action-puzzle are a gift if they still feel intended (e.g., letting the engineer bait the sentry themselves if WOLF refuses).

### F14. Limbo playtesting (Playdead) [PUZZLES-16]
Game Developer (staff byline), 24 Feb 2012 (Dino Patti, Arnt Jensen): "several hundred people" tested; about 150 of them were asked "to simply play it for the first time without any direction"; Jensen: "We just give them the controller, and then we sat behind them, and we didn't ask any questions." Goal: "Could they solve the puzzles? What should we do to make it easier to solve them?"
WOLF//OVERRIDE: silent first-play observation with a note of where players stop moving for more than ~10-20 s; that list is the hint and readability backlog. Never ask leading questions.

### F15. Portal postmortem (Valve) [PUZZLES-17]
Game Developer, 4 Nov 2008, byline "Kim Swift", described as "an article written by the game's creators" (reprint of the January 2008 Game Developer magazine postmortem; the co-authorship of Erik Wolpaw and Jeep Barnett is not shown on the page: UNVERIFIED): "Every week (or any time we created a significant new piece of content), we would bring in someone that hadn't played the game before." When playtesters "would get stuck in this section for a very long time" on the double energy-ball redirect, the team broke it into separate sections with a single redirect first. Game data box: 8 full-time developers, 0 contractors, 26 months.
WOLF//OVERRIDE: when a step confuses, split it into a taught single step before the combined step (e.g., teach "overload makes things blow" on something small before the door).

### F16. Accessibility contrast numbers [PUZZLES-18][PUZZLES-19]
Xbox Accessibility Guideline 102 (Microsoft devdocs, accessed 2026-10-12): standard text or visual element 4.5:1; large-scale 3:1; high-contrast mode 7:1 for all UI elements; key gameplay elements use the same ratios. Game Accessibility Guidelines, "Give a clear indication that interactive elements are interactive" (cognitive and vision, intermediate): "clear and consistent differences in style, contrast, or additional signifiers such as a mouseover effect, glow, icon etc."
WOLF//OVERRIDE: target >= 3:1 luminance contrast between a live interactable's signifier (lamp, handle edge) and its immediate background in the darkest room at default brightness, checked on a phone capture; >= 4.5:1 for the prompt text. Optional high-contrast mode >= 7:1.

### F17. Carlsen, Limbo: Balancing Fun and Frustration in Puzzle Design (GDC Europe 2010) [PUZZLES-20][PUZZLES-21]
Report by Brandon Sheffield, Game Developer, 16 Aug 2010; session page on GDC Vault. Beyond what the 25-games study already quoted ("It's very important that the correct solution is fairly easy to execute"): Limbo's team found that showing the trap in the same scene as the spider "caused players to focus on the trap, not the spider, so they moved it away to another screen"; "they based cues on sound, you can hear when the trap falls"; "the wrong approaches should clearly present that they are wrong"; "Limbo is linear, so you can get really stuck, and there's no hint system." FACT-CHECK: these four passages are Sheffield's paraphrase of the talk, not Carlsen's own words; Carlsen's direct quotes nearby are "It takes the player a long time to go back to an idea that he had previously discarded as not being possible" and, on having no hints, "That's not really a fortunate situation to put the player in, but we didn't want to have any in-game menus or anything." Carlsen did say "You could call it a learning-by-dying game" and "We wanted the player to die a lot in this game", and stressed "It's important that you also treat him nicely" ("You don't ever really get penalized for dying").
WOLF//OVERRIDE: (a) don't show a decoy interactable next to the real problem — if the drop-arm panel is visible while the player is still at the valve, players fixate on it early; gate its lamp until the sentry wakes. (b) Use sound as the "state changed" cue (breaker clunk, line hum rising, valve hiss) — this matches the procedural audio direction. (c) Limbo's "learning by dying" is a deliberate counter-model: WOLF//OVERRIDE's Secure-the-Evidence rule makes deaths costlier than Limbo's, so the puzzle steps must be learnable WITHOUT dying (Gilbert rule) and death must stay reserved for hazards that were telegraphed.

### F19. God of War Ragnarök hint timing and the option to reduce them [PUZZLES-6][PUZZLES-24][PUZZLES-25]
Matt Sophos via eXputer (17 Feb 2023): first hint "too aggressive". Official: PlayStation Blog, Grace Orlady (Santa Monica Studio communications director), 18 Sep 2024: Reduced Puzzle Hints, "enabling this will reduce the frequency with which you hear puzzle hints from your companions." WOLF//OVERRIDE: first WOLF hint no earlier than ~45-60 s of no progress on that node (derived, between the ~20-30 s one reader complained about and TLG's ~5 min from the 25-games card; validate by play), with a settings toggle (Hints: Normal / Fewer / Off).

### F20. Hint systems can reduce persistence: Refraction study [PUZZLES-26]
O'Rourke, Ballweber, Popović, "Hint Systems May Negatively Impact Performance in Educational Games", ACM Learning at Scale 2014. 50,000 students (10,000 sampled per condition), 4 hint designs vs no-hint baseline: "all four hint systems negatively impacted performance". Baseline won intro levels 89.45% of the time, better than all hint conditions. Cites earlier work (Andersen et al., CHI 2012 [PUZZLES-42]): adding a help button to Refraction "caused players to complete 12% fewer levels and play for 15% less time, even though only 31% of players actually clicked the button". Earned hints slightly raised persistence (median 192 s vs 178 s for embedded hints). Caveat: children, educational game, browser.
FACT-CHECK addition from the primary Andersen et al. paper [PUZZLES-42]: the help-button effect depended on the game. In Foldit (the most complex of the three games) "providing a help button in this case increased engagement. Players played about 12% longer with a help button than without"; in Hello Worlds players returned about 3% less often; only in Refraction did it cut progress 12% and time 15%. So "a visible help button reduces persistence" holds for simple puzzle games, not universally.
WOLF//OVERRIDE: don't put a visible HINT button on screen; let hints arrive diegetically and late from WOLF, and make the first tier a nudge (look/sniff at the right object) not an answer.

### F22. Working memory ~4 chunks [PUZZLES-29]
Cowan, "The magical number 4 in short-term memory", Behavioral and Brain Sciences 24(1):87-114, 2001: "A single, central capacity limit averaging about four chunks is implicated". 
WOLF//OVERRIDE: derived rule: no more than 3-4 LIVE interactables (or puzzle-relevant moving parts) visible at once in a camera view; set dressing must not read as interactable. The Junction's USE points run from the breaker (x 200) to the hatch (x 880), a 680 px span, while the gameplay view at zoom 1.35 is 960/1.35 = ~711 px wide (main.gd). FACT-CHECK correction: the draft said "~900 px, about 1.3 views, so 4-5 per view". Because the span is narrower than one view, all 6-7 USE points can be on screen together when the camera is centred on the room, which is OVER the 3-4 target, not at it (camera clamping not checked in play; confirm with a render.sh capture). Gate lamps so only the next 1-2 are live.

### F25. Valve: Portal commentary (2007) [PUZZLES-32]

### F26. Valve: playtesters aren't idiots (Faliszek, 2008) [PUZZLES-33]
Game Developer, Chris Remo, 7 Aug 2008: in a Left 4 Dead internal (Valve staff) playtest, "a texture behind a ladder had been altered. 'It was the minorest of changes!' ... 'Nobody saw the ladder. The entire playtest broke. Everybody got stuck on the third map'". "Playtesting is just proof to you. There's no way you can argue with that."

### F27. Platform touch-target numbers (fills the 25-games "touch target sizes" gap) [PUZZLES-34][PUZZLES-35]
Apple HIG Accessibility (accessed 2026-10-12): iOS/iPadOS default control size 44x44 pt, minimum 28x28 pt; macOS 28x28 / 20x20 pt; "about 12 points of padding around elements that include a bezel" and "about 24 points" for elements without one; "Augment audio cues with visual cues. This is especially important for games". Android Accessibility Help: "at least 48x48dp, separated by 8dp of space or more"; 48x48dp is "about 9mm".
WOLF//OVERRIDE: on phones, do not make players tap the world object (pixel hunting by finger); use a contextual USE button >= 44 pt / 48 dp with >= 8-12 pt gaps, anchored away from the interactable so the thumb never covers it; the world object shows the signifier, the button does the action.

Wii, Volume 1, page 6 "A Medal for Skilled Players" (Satoru Iwata with Shigeru Miyamoto): the Super Guide hint block first appeared "after losing three turns", which upset Miyamoto when he played it ("Are you making fun of me!?"). The team debated "Should it be five times, or would it be better if it were ten times?" and settled on eight: "after losing eight turns, the hint block should appear", reasoning that a player who fails a level "over a couple of sessions" should be allowed to move on. Skilled players who never trigger it get medals on the title screen. Page 7 "A Block Floating In Mid-Air Is Unnatural" (Miyamoto): "Even when I was working on Donkey Kong, I'd design things properly. I'd make sure that if there was a floor here, then there'd be a pillar somewhere to support it."; the lone floating block of Super Mario Bros. prompted "Just what is this supposed to be hanging from?"; NSMB Wii's team argued at length over what looked "natural" and "unnatural" (e.g., fireballs entering water).
WOLF//OVERRIDE: (a) Separate the TELEMETRY flag from the IN-GAME OFFER. More than ~3 deaths at one node is a design-review flag in test builds (DERIVED; see F30), but an in-game "skip this step" offer should not pop up that early: Nintendo found 3 failures too early and patronising and chose 8. The pause-menu Skip stays available at any time (TLOU2 model), with a proactive offer only after repeated failures (somewhere in Nintendo's 5-10 range; validate by play). (b) Miyamoto's Donkey Kong rule is a primary-source statement of Nathanial's "no hovering items" rule: every platform, pipe, tank and panel in a realistic base needs a visible support (bracket, hanger, pillar, wall mount), or it reads as unnatural.

## Synthesis for WOLF//OVERRIDE

### How many interactables per view?
No shipped game publishes a count. Converging evidence: working memory about four chunks (Cowan); "almost no extraneous elements" (Brown); difficulty rises with "the number of options the player can choose from at each moment" (Square Enix Montreal via Brown); Limbo moved the trap away from the spider so players stopped fixating on it (Carlsen). DERIVED TARGET: at most 3-4 LIVE interactables in a camera view, and only 1-2 of those relevant to the current step; everything else in the view must read as set dressing (dark lamp, tagged out, out of reach, or clearly background depth). Validate by play.

### How the player is told what is interactable (layered, cheapest first)
1. Diegetic state: lamp lit / dark, sound (hum, hiss, clunk), motion (needle, pulsing tag). Same visual language on every interactable (Blow: know instantly it is a puzzle; Kasurinen: consistent rules; Lundeen: one recognisable surface).
2. Light and composition: warm key on the live object, lines (conduit, shadows) pointing at it (Jensvold; Portal button-to-door lines).
3. Companion body language: WOLF looks at / walks to / sniffs the next node (Six, Trico, Alyx pointing).
4. In-range prompt only (25-games study, Stray), world-anchored, >= 4.5:1 contrast (XAG 102).
5. Optional accessibility: high-contrast interactables (TLOU2), hint frequency setting (GoW Ragnarök), hold-to-toggle, skip puzzle (TLOU2).

### Proposed hint ladder (DERIVED; numbers to validate by play)
Timer = seconds with no progress on the current node of the dependency chain (F11), reset on any progress; never during a chase or choice.
- T0 on entry: WOLF states the GOAL (not the steps), then steps aside (Alyx).
- T1 ~45-60 s: WOLF body-language nudge toward the node (look, sniff, ear flick) + the node's sound pulses.
- T2 ~90-120 s: one short WOLF line naming the object, not the action.
- T3 ~180 s or on request (USE on WOLF): explicit line. Pause menu: Skip this step.
- Settings: Hints Normal / Fewer / Off (GoW Ragnarök), separate from WOLF trust (hints are never withheld as a trust penalty — accessibility must not carry a moral cost, per project rules).
Bounds: GoW companions ~20-30 s drew complaints (one Push Square reader comment, ANECDOTAL; poll there: 40% of 1,521 voters found companion puzzle talk "annoying and patronising") and Sophos called it "too aggressive"; Valve's nagging became hated after "about three minutes" of repetition; TLG ~5 min was too slow (25-games card). Refraction (Andersen et al. 2012; O'Rourke et al. 2014) suggests no always-visible HINT button for a simple puzzle game, though in complex Foldit a help button raised play time ~12%.

### Fairness rules (no moon logic, no pixel hunting, no trial-and-error deaths)
- Problem before tool (Gilbert backwards puzzles); goal clear, only the HOW is hidden (Brown).
- Solutions read as real maintenance logic of a machine base (Brown problem-solving; engineer's job).
- Every wrong attempt answers "no, never, because" (Carlsen 2023; Limbo "wrong approaches should clearly present that they are wrong").
- No death needed to learn (Gilbert). Lethal hazards are shown working on something else, or from safety, before the player can enter them.
- Timing windows repeat (Brown on Braid); the sentry "crosses the mark on every pass" is right.
- Anything that looks operable works or reads as dead (Norman signifiers; Stray criticism in 25-games study). No floating props, no doors that don't open, no fake handles.
- Execution easy (Carlsen 2010; Brown "effortless to actually execute"); crank holds get a toggle (TLOU2).
- Test with fresh players silently (Limbo ~150 fresh; Portal weekly; Teikari blind to difficulty; Faliszek ladder texture).

### Current-build observations (from code, 2026-10-12; not played)
- JUNCTION_ENTRY_LINE spells the whole chain on entry ("The breaker by the entrance feeds that line. Overload it, crank the valve, and the door goes."). For an action set piece this is defensible, but it is the GoW/Horizon pattern; consider WOLF stating only the goal ("that door has to go") and reading the gauge, letting the player connect breaker -> line -> valve, with the full line moved to T2/T3. Nathanial's call.
- MANUAL BYPASS: 8 ticks x 0.4 s = 3.2 s hold; part-crank persists ("time, never progress") — good; add hold-to-toggle.
- Junction has ~6-7 USE points across a 680 px span, inside one ~711 px view; gate lamps so only the next 1-2 are live.
- BAIT_NO_LINE already promises a repeating window — good (Braid lesson).

## Agreement / disagreement with design-reference-25-games.md
- AGREE: zero persistent HUD, prompts only in USE range (its §7 target). Add: an optional high-contrast interactable mode (TLOU2 [5]; XAG 102 [18]) and >= 4.5:1 prompt contrast.
- AGREE: "teach and steer by placement; show the goal first" (§3.5) — supported by Gilbert [2], Brown [9], Hayashida [11], Portal [32].
- AGREE: "Prompt-gated jumps that always land (but make visibly climbable things climbable)" — generalise to every object: looks operable => works, or visibly dead (Norman [1]).
- AGREE: holds should be toggleable, never auto-completed (§9) — TLOU2 "every button hold into a toggle" [5].
- AGREE: WOLF never rescues and never solves for the player — Valve's Alyx "doesn't actually solve the puzzle" [31] and "stated a goal and then stepped aside" [30].
- FILLS GAP: exact Valve wording on Alyx nagging ("after about three minutes ... make the player hate Alyx") and the hint-on-request lesson [30]; touch target sizes (Apple 44x44 pt default, 28x28 min, 12/24 pt padding; Android 48x48 dp ~9 mm, 8 dp gaps) [34][35].
- PARTLY DISAGREE: its stuck-player row treats "the engineer-only path is visible in the room" as the primary fallback and an idle nudge as secondary. Those solve different problems: the engineer-only path covers WOLF REFUSING; it does nothing for a player who does not understand the junction chain. A puzzle-readability need is a separate, tiered, state-aware hint ladder (WOLF body language first, words later, explicit on request), with timings bounded by GoW Ragnarök (too early) [6][24][25], Valve (nagging hated by ~3 min) [30] and TLG (~5 min, too slow; its own card) — and no visible HINT button (Refraction: -12% levels, -15% time from merely adding a help button) [26].
- PARTLY DISAGREE: it lists "fresh-tester readability play-tests" under LATER. Evidence says NOW and repeatedly: Limbo ~150 fresh one-shot testers [16]; Portal a new tester every week [17]; Teikari "blind to the difficulty" as a solo designer [15]; L4D ladder lost by one texture change [33]. With the art redo in progress, every repaint can break readability.
- NUANCE on Carlsen: the study quotes his "easy to execute" rule; the same talk also says Limbo was "a learning-by-dying game" with "no hint system" [20]. WOLF//OVERRIDE's evidence-drop death rule makes deaths costlier than Limbo's, so Limbo's trial-and-death model must NOT be copied for puzzle steps; Gilbert's no-death-to-learn rule [2] applies.
