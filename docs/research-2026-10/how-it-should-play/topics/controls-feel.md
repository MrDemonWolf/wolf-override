## Findings

### F1. Side-scroller camera patterns and numbers (Itay Keren) [CONTROLS-FEEL-1][CONTROLS-FEEL-2]
- What it says: Keren (Mushroom 11) catalogues ~20 side-scroller camera behaviours: position-locking, edge-snapping, camera-window, platform-snapping, lerp-smoothing, physics-smoothing, forward-focus (look-ahead), dual forward-focus, position-snapping, projected-focus (velocity-extrapolated), target-focus (follows input intent), cue-focus / attractors (incl. Limbo), region-based focus, gesture-focus, zoom-to-fit, cinematic paths, speedup push zones. The three jobs of a camera are "Attention, Interaction and Comfort". On comfort: "conflicting sensory signals (Visual vs. Vestibular) may lead to discomfort and nausea". Lerp-smoothing is "most effective and ubiquitous". Physics smoothing "takes into account existing camera speed, which means that it moves into action slightly slower" (Never Alone). Region-based focus (Vessel) with player-based shifts achieves "a good combination of preset framing with solid perceived control". Camera-window: the character moves within a window and the camera scrolls "only ... when it pushes against the edges of that window" (Keren's comfort argument; "jarring" is a paraphrase, not his word).
- Numbers: Defender focuses "around 25% screen-width forward" of the ship; Super Mario Bros. uses a one-sided camera-window plus "a virtual point, around 25% off-center, where the camera speeds up to catch up with Mario" (a speed-up pull zone, NOT a 25% forward focus — fact-check fix); Super Mario World's dual forward-focus keeps the target point "until Mario hits a predefined threshold" before locking to the other anchor (threshold value not given in the article), plus platform-snapping; Bonanza Bros. switches at "around double" the player's speed; Geometry Wars region motion at half speed. The talk itself (GDC 2015 Independent Games Summit) is listed as free content on GDC Vault (checked 2026-10-12; video not watched); the Game Developer article is Keren's own modified write-up (May 11, 2015).
- Limbo is cited for cue focus: the camera uses attractor objects; "By manipulating exposure, positioning and zooming, it creates a truly unique ambience."
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: our camera is pure position-locking plus lerp-smoothing (Godot position_smoothing_speed 4.0), no look-ahead, no window. Keren's patterns that fit a slow cinematic walk: (a) a small camera-window (dead zone) so small steps at a station (settle_x sidesteps, crank stance) never move the camera (comfort + jitter); (b) dual forward-focus with a direction threshold, so a quick turn to look back does not swing the camera; (c) cue-focus attractors on the sentry, the exit door and WOLF when he scouts ahead (Limbo precedent; keeps the 2.5D framing "authored" without taking control away); (d) region-based anchors per room for the Blowback beat. 25% of our 711 px view = ~178 px forward, which matches the sentry's 180 px sight range — a strong argument for some look-ahead in the Junction, but full Defender-style 25% is a shooter value; a slow walker needs less (see F9 for Godot and the derived target).

### F2. Celeste's forgiveness mechanics and the real numbers in its released source [CONTROLS-FEEL-3][CONTROLS-FEEL-4]
- What it says: Thorson lists coyote time, jump buffering, half gravity at the apex while holding jump, jump and dash corner correction, semi-solid popping, lift-momentum storage, a wall jump that works 2 px from a wall (super wall jump ~5 px), stamina refunds. "All are centered around widening timing or positioning windows, so that everything is fudged a tiny bit in the player's favor." "...Celeste can feel kind even though it's very difficult — it wants you to succeed." Originally a Twitter thread (the page says so; its date, c. 2020, is UNVERIFIED).
- Numbers from the official Player.cs (Maddy Thorson and Noel Berry released it on GitHub in 2018 as a learning resource): JumpGraceTime (coyote) 0.1 s = 6 frames at 60 fps; UpwardCornerCorrection and DashCornerCorrection 4 px; MaxRun 90 px/s, RunAccel 1000 px/s², so 0 to full run in 0.09 s (~5-6 frames) and, because stopping uses the same RunAccel when no direction is held, full run to stop in 0.09 s too; reversing 90 to -90 takes ~0.18 s; AirMult 0.65 (less control in the air); RunReduce 400 px/s² only bleeds speed above the max; WalkSpeed 64 (cutscene walk). Gravity 900, MaxFall 160, JumpSpeed -105, VarJumpTime 0.2 s, DashTime 0.15 s.
- The jump-buffer length lives in Input.cs, which was not released: UNVERIFIED (unofficial decompiles give 0.08 s; not used here).
- The Player readme: "In a platformer like Celeste, the player behavior code needs to be very tightly ordered and tuned"; they "never implemented more than a simple frame-by-frame sprite component" — i.e. physics drives, frame-by-frame animation follows.
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: there are no jumps, but the same idea applies to every USE: (a) an interaction buffer — a USE pressed a few frames before the engineer enters a station's range (or while a line is finishing) should still fire on arrival; (b) "interaction coyote" — a USE pressed just after walking out of range should still count; (c) generous ranges, never pixel-precise standing spots (the code already auto-steps the engineer to crank spots, which is the same "fudge in the player's favour"). Celeste's ~0.09 s start/stop is the twitchy-platformer end of the scale; a cinematic walker can be heavier but must stay inside the budgets in F4.

### F3. Steve Swink, Game Feel (2008): the definition and the time thresholds [CONTROLS-FEEL-5][CONTROLS-FEEL-6][CONTROLS-FEEL-7][CONTROLS-FEEL-8][CONTROLS-FEEL-30]
- Read chapter 1 ("Defining Game Feel", pp. 1-33) as a scanned excerpt hosted on a course site; the book is Morgan Kaufmann / Elsevier 2008 (CRC/Routledge reissue). Definition (p. 6, bold in the book): "Real-time control of virtual objects in a simulated space, with interactions emphasized by polish." Real-time control is "more like driving a car" than a conversation: "The result of input feels as though it is perceived in the same moment it's expressed" (pp. 3-4). Polish matters as much as simulation: "for players—simulation and polish are indistinguishable" (p. 6); De Blob's squash shader changed the feel "with no changes to the physics at all" (p. 6). Five experiences of game feel (p. 10): aesthetic sensation of control, pleasure of learning a skill, extension of the senses, extension of identity, interaction with a unique physical reality. Starcraft/Diablo are borderline because clicks set a destination rather than steering continuously (p. 9).
- The millisecond thresholds are in chapter 2 ("Game Feel and Human Perception"), which I could NOT open (Taylor & Francis returned 403; Google Books blocked). Secondary but consistent: Pichlmair & Johansen (IEEE Transactions on Games, DOI 10.1109/TG.2021.3072241; arXiv preprint Nov 2020) quote Swink (they cite the book as Morgan Kaufmann, 2009): "real-time control relies on sustaining three time thresholds: the impression of motion, perceived instantaneous response and continuity of response". Liz England's 2015 review relays Swink's correction cycle (player reads feedback, decides, acts; the game reads and responds) with the computer's half "under 100 milliseconds". The ~240 ms human correction cycle (Card, Moran & Newell's Model Human Processor: perceptual ~100 ms [50-200], cognitive ~70 ms, motor ~70 ms) appeared only in a search snippet: UNVERIFIED in a source I opened.
- Swink's 2007 Gamasutra article: Mario accelerates gradually "from rest to his maximum speed" with deceleration simulating friction and inertia; polish includes particles, screen shake and view shifts that "convey the physical properties of objects through their motion and interaction". His 2006 "Principles of Virtual Sensation": "If rotating the steering wheel of your car meant turning thirty seconds from now, there would be no control or mastery."
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: the computer's half of every loop (press -> first visible/audible response) must stay under ~100 ms, i.e. ~6 frames at 60 fps, INCLUDING engine/display latency. That leaves only a few frames for any authored "wind-up" before something visibly answers: a hold-to-crank, a walk start, a WOLF request must show a first response (hand moves to handle, ear turns, sound) inside that budget even if the full animation takes longer. Polish (footsteps, dust, rattle) is not optional decoration: it is half of what the player reads as "weight".

### F4. Measured input-to-screen latency in shipped games (Mick West, 2008) [CONTROLS-FEEL-9][CONTROLS-FEEL-10]
- What it says: West (Neversoft co-founder) filmed the controller and the TV with a 60 fps camera and counted frames from press to on-screen response. Best case is 3 frames (50 ms) at 60 fps and 6 frames (100 ms) at 30 fps. Measured: PS3 menus 50 ms; Guitar Hero III 50 ms; Ridge Racer 7, Virtua Tennis 3, Ninja Gaiden Sigma, PixelJunk Racers 67 ms; Genji 100 ms; Tony Hawk's Proving Ground and BlackSite 133 ms; Halo 3 133-167 ms; Skate and GTA IV 167 ms; Harry Potter OotP 167-233 ms; Heavenly Sword 117-300 ms. Conclusion: "Games that run at 60fps all seem to have a response time to 4/60ths, and while 3/60ths is possible, 4/60ths is a very good response time." At 30 fps aim for 6/60ths "and certainly not slip below 8/60ths."
- "Programming Responsiveness" (July 9, 2008): lag piles up from input polling time within the frame, logic order (e.g. velocity applied after physics), CPU render setup, GPU buffering, display flip and animation events; three stacked mistakes give six frames, "potentially ... from six to eight frames, which would be quite unplayable, even at 60 frames per second", and six frames at 30 fps is "an unbearable 1/5th of a second, or 200 milliseconds". Animation is a named culprit: velocity impulses placed mid-animation for looks, or animation updated in render, delay the response; West's fix is to put the impulse on the animation's first frame. Players "cannot always put into words" what is wrong and say the game felt "slow" or "not tight"; reviews call such games "'sluggish,' 'unresponsive,' 'floaty,' or 'sloppy.'" (fact-check: wording corrected; "floaty" comes from reviews, not testers). Recommendation: "be able to clearly describe the frame-by-frame path through logic and rendering that a button-triggered action takes before it creates visual feedback."
- Second fact-check: West's per-game numbers are CRT-equivalent. He measured on a plasma and subtracted its 2/60 s (PS3 menus were 3/60 on a CRT and 5/60 on the plasma), and he warns that flat panels "add an additional 2 frames of lag". So 50-67 ms is the game's own share; on a typical flat panel the same games land at ~83-100 ms end to end, which matches the ~100 ms 'instant' ceiling. 30 fps games ran 100-167 ms typical, up to 233 ms (Harry Potter OotP) and 300 ms (Heavenly Sword).
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: target press-to-first-pixel <= 4 frames (67 ms, game share, display excluded) on desktop at 60 fps and <= ~100 ms on phones (touch adds its own latency, F10). Document the frame path for MOVE and USE: Godot polls input, `_physics_process` sets velocity, then render; Godot's own guide notes physics interpolation "will increase input lag", triple-buffered V-Sync adds frames, and the game's 30 FPS option doubles the floor to 100 ms. A slow cinematic walk start must still begin *something* (lean, foot lift, sound) in the first 1-2 frames; never wait for an animation event to start velocity.

### F5. Godot 4.7 specifics for latency and the current camera (official docs and source) [CONTROLS-FEEL-11][CONTROLS-FEEL-12][CONTROLS-FEEL-13]
- Godot's "Fixing jitter, stutter and input lag" page: double-buffered V-Sync via Swapchain Image Count 2 (since 4.3) cuts lag, but only "When using the Forward+ or Mobile rendering methods" (the default is triple-buffered). Second fact-check: project.godot sets rendering_method and rendering_method.mobile to gl_compatibility, so this lever does NOT apply to WOLF//OVERRIDE as configured; raise Physics Ticks Per Second above 60 (120/180/240 are "good starting points") especially with physics interpolation, which "improves smoothness but increases latency"; `Input.set_use_accumulated_input(false)` processes input every event at some CPU cost; cap FPS slightly below refresh.
- Camera2D: drag margins (default 0.2 of the half-screen per side, off by default) implement Keren's camera-window; `drag_horizontal_offset` shifts the camera within the margins (a ready-made look-ahead knob); position smoothing is first-order exponential: in camera_2d.cpp (4.7.2-stable, line ~202) `c = position_smoothing_speed * delta; smoothed += (target - smoothed) * c`.
- Fact-check addition (code, main.gd:435 and 747-750): every room is 960 px wide and the camera x is clamped to [355.5, 604.5], so the camera can travel only ~249 px in total. Within ~355 px of either wall the clamp, not the follow, decides framing, so any look-ahead only acts in the middle ~249 px of a room. Look-ahead targets below must be checked against that range (a 178 px look-ahead would be ~70% of it).
- Derived numbers for OUR camera (speed 4.0, 60 fps): time constant 0.25 s; a step change settles 95% in ~0.73 s; while the engineer walks at a steady 190 px/s the camera trails ~44 px BEHIND them (about 6% of the 711 px view), so the player sees less of what is ahead than of what is behind — the opposite of look-ahead. At the planned walk 80 px/s the trail is ~19 px.
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: (a) the cheapest look-ahead is to target `engineer_x + facing * L` instead of `engineer_x`, with L eased (not snapped) on a turn, plus a small dead zone; (b) do not rely on smoothing speed alone; (c) keep camera code in one place (`_update_gameplay_camera`).

### F6. How Playdead kept INSIDE's boy responsive while animated (primary: Playdead's own slides) [CONTROLS-FEEL-14][CONTROLS-FEEL-29]
- Source: "Animation & Computerspil" slides by Andreas N. Grøntved (animator) and Martin Fasterholdt (gameplay designer), Danish, linked from Playdead's official publications repo as "Animation-talk in gdocs". Undated (INSIDE shipped 2016; slides say Grøntved spent 5 years on it). I read the exported text and translated; paraphrases below, short translated quotes in quotation marks.
- Numbers: Grøntved was the sole animator on INSIDE, 3,500+ animations, ~2,000 of them for the boy; "many animations are no longer than 1 second"; a 20-rung ladder uses a two-step loop, not 20 steps; 40+ "themes" (sneak, panic, balance, hurt, tired) swap the boy's default animation set; 6 jump variants for variety.
- Principles (translated): For INSIDE "the player should always be in direct control" and "animations must feel like one flowing movement" — "two conflicting goals", "animation vs. reaction, an eternal dilemma"; they want the best of both "but fast, direct response is favoured". Button example: the animator wants the hand to reach naturally, "BUT the button must be pressed NOW, because that is what the player imagines happens"; the player already felt the impulse travel to their fingers and should not have to watch it happen again on screen.
- Techniques: short clips stitched in code; walk/run loops kept "faceless" so repeated details don't stick out; blends between clips (a jog comes "free" from blending fast walk and run); hand-made transition clips where a blend looks silly, especially 180° turns; almost every animation is built to handle three inputs — left, right, none — because "animations are constantly interrupted by the player, because the player has a will"; input is locked "as little as possible", accepted mainly when something violent happens to the boy; the jump is violent enough to just play out. Nuance (fact-check addition, translated): when the stick is held right through a landing, "we only allow the boy to run when he looks like he can! If the boy looks like he is doing everything he can to move on, it's OK" — i.e. INSIDE does sometimes delay velocity, but only while the body visibly answers the input with effort. The boy runs in place and "the code moves him forward" (no root motion), and he predicts ground contact, landings and ledges. Additive layers: breathing (driven by the audio system), a look-around system that turns spine and head toward points placed in the world, IK feet and hands. Every rejected attempt is answered "with an animation and a hint" at the right solution.
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: this is the template for the planned walk 80 / jog 190 and painted frame-by-frame cycles: code owns position and speed; the painted cycle plays in place with playback rate = speed / stride (no foot slide); a turn may get a short authored turn clip but velocity reverses immediately; USE fires the effect on the press frame (the handle starts moving now) while the reach is a short overlay, never a gate. Lock input only for violent beats (knockdown, blast). Every refused/wrong USE (a sealed door, a dead panel) gets a small authored response plus a hint, never silence. The engineer's look-at toward WOLF, the sentry or a live panel is a cheap, high-value additive layer (already a decided "real-feel" item).

### F7. Shipping INSIDE on touch: animations had to get shorter (Playdead, Digital Dragons 2018) [CONTROLS-FEEL-15]
- Source: "INSIDE shipping on iOS" deck (pptx in Playdead's publications repo; deck author metadata Mikkel Gjøl; video at Digital Dragons 2018). Bonus slides 91-96 and their speaker notes.
- What it says: two touch modes — Split (left half = stick, right half = swipes/hold) and Fluid (any finger, brief hold+move = stick, long hold+move = grab/drag); "all touches/fingers are treated equally... If one finger moves right and another left, the boy will stand still"; the device is held with a thumb resting on screen. Swipe toward an object to grab it (only objects that block movement). After grabbing, "a swipe is often enough to complete an interaction"; "Compared to quick swipe-gestures the animation often felt too slow." Auto-complete was tried and rejected: it "ends up waiting for triggered animation to end. loses feeling of interaction". They chose "Short as possible animation while still prioritising the feeling of interaction" — a "Very painful change, since we really liked the weight of the controller input", with a "Balance between matching the swipe, while not looking too instantaneous", and a "Challenging change when timing was important (Sentry gun in city, Sentry gun in sewers)" (fact-check: the deck says "challenging", not "hardest"). Console 1080p60 vs iOS 30 fps (60 on Apple TV 4K).
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: our hold-to-crank (8 ticks x 0.4 s = 3.2 s) and the junction valve need a touch-tuned version: keep the crank interactive (each tick visibly answers the held finger) rather than auto-completing, and consider fewer/shorter ticks on touch. The sentry/tank timing is exactly where Playdead struggled; test it on a phone (the design reference already says so — agreed).

### F8. Restraint in effects: "Turn it down 90%" (Playdead VFX, Control Conference 2016) [CONTROLS-FEEL-16]
- Source: Mikkel Svendsen, "Turn It Down 90% — INSIDE VFX", Control Conference 2016; pptx with speaker notes in Playdead's publications repo (the design reference saw only the title; this fills that gap).
- What it says (Svendsen was a Playdead VFX artist; "VFX lead" is not stated in the deck): mentors' feedback on his effects "was mostly 'Turn it down 90%'"; "Turning down, things usually look better"; rain was turned down "till it feels uncomfortable" (advice from a colleague) and was better for it; "effects can't be distracting"; "The best CGI is that which we don't even know is there". Slides: "Blend into the picture", "De-detail the details", "Delete, delete, delete", "Maybe we don't need 10.000" crowd particles. Water displacement is "Seen for about 5-10 frames per dive". The deck closes on a counterweight (slide 51, "TURN IT UP 90%"; notes): "we do turn it up to 11, once. Sometimes, you can totally turn it up 90%." — restraint everywhere so one climax can go full strength (fact-check addition).
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: supports the existing impact budget (full-strength shake/flash only at climaxes, toggles). Practical rule: tune every new effect to where it feels right, then cut it until it feels slightly too weak, and keep that unless a playtest says otherwise. Dust, steam and sparks should never be the brightest thing on screen; contact effects can last 5-10 frames.

### F9. Juice values from Vlambeer (Nuclear Throne, via Nijman's own words) and the trauma model (Eiserloh) [CONTROLS-FEEL-17][CONTROLS-FEEL-18][CONTROLS-FEEL-19][CONTROLS-FEEL-20]
- Nijman's talk "The art of screenshake" (INDIGO Classes 2013, ~40 min, YouTube) is the canonical source but I could not read its contents (YouTube page gave only the title): its specific list is UNVERIFIED here.
- Nijman's own numbers for one Nuclear Throne pistol shot (RPS interview by Graham Smith; RPS blocks our fetcher, so read as quoted by Tom Armitage, infovore.org, 22 Oct 2013): game runs 30 fps at 320x240; shell ejected at 2-4 px/frame; bullet at 16 px/frame with 0-4° spread; "We then kick the camera back 6 pixels from where you are aiming, and 'add 4 to the screenshake'"; shake "degenerates quickly"; weapon kick 2 px then slides back; a 10-20 ms freeze on impact; enemy knockback 3 px/frame. Scale: 6 px camera kick = 1.9% of a 320 px screen; if the "+4" screenshake is in pixels (the quote does not give its unit: ASSUMPTION), 4 px = 1.25%.
- Squirrel Eiserloh, "Math for Game Programmers: Juicing Your Cameras With Math" (GDC 2016, free on GDC's YouTube): covers framing, smoothed motion, camera shake and split-screen. The trauma model (trauma 0..1 decays over time, shake = trauma² or ³, Perlin/smooth noise, translational + rotational in 2D) is how the talk is widely implemented (e.g. KidsCanCode Godot recipe crediting the talk); the exact slide values are UNVERIFIED.
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: `impact.gd` already implements trauma² shake with noise and a 0.05 time-scale hit-stop capped at 0.1 s. Nijman's 10-20 ms freeze is per bullet hit in a shooter; our hit-stops are 0.06 s on the knockdown (`main.gd` `_fail_beat`), 0.07 s on the door blast (`_blast_feedback`) and 0.09 s on the tank drop (main.gd:2570) — 3-9x longer, and they slow time to 0.05x rather than freezing it. That is appropriate only for those three rare heavy beats, never for routine events (fact-check: the 0.07 and 0.09 s values were missing). Our MAX_SHAKE_PX 10 world px at zoom 1.35 = ~13.5 screen px of 960 = ~1.4% of width — similar in proportion to ONE Nuclear Throne pistol shot, so it is not over the top as a peak, but Playdead's restraint (F8) argues the common case should be a fraction of it.

### F10. Animation priority measured in Prince of Persia's own 1989 source (Jordan Mechner) [CONTROLS-FEEL-21][CONTROLS-FEEL-22][CONTROLS-FEEL-23]
- Source: Mechner's Apple II 6502 source, posted by him on GitHub in 2012 (`SEQTABLE.S`, the animation sequence table). Each `db` entry is one displayed frame; `chx` is the horizontal move for that frame.
- Counts (read directly): `startrun` = 6 frames before the run cycle, and the first 3 move the Prince 0 px (`chx` starts on frame 4); the run cycle = 8 frames; `runstop` = 8 frames (with small forward moves) before `stand`; standing `turn` = an about-face plus 8 frames; `runturn` = 13 frames of skid-and-turn before the run resumes. Fact-check addition: even PoP had a cancel window — `turnrun` ("from frame 48") lets a standing turn break into `startrun` after its 4th frame instead of playing all 8. At the ~15 fps Mechner aimed for ("when the frames are run in sequence at 15 fps, it'll create an illusion of life", journal quote via Animation Obsessive 2026) that is ~0.4 s to start, ~0.53 s to stop, ~0.87 s to reverse from a run (exact shipped frame rate UNVERIFIED; at 12 fps the times are 25% longer).
- Why it worked: Mechner "really wanted the character to feel human so that, as players, we would identify with the little guy" (Ubisoft News, 2024); Bio Jade Adam Granger, creative director of Ubisoft's in-development Sands of Time remake: "Every jump has tension built into it." ... "Prince of Persia, where you are rooted in this world with the weight of that character." The game is built around that commitment (careful steps, run-ups), so the slowness is a rule the level design respects, not lag the player fights.
- Another World (Eric Chahi, GDC 2011 classic postmortem, reported by Christian Nutt for Game Developer, March 3, 2011): rotoscoped vector animation; the Amiga running polygons at "around 20 frames per second" was "a major turning point"; "The real media is the player's memory and imagination, so seeing it once is enough". Run is a held modifier (Wikipedia; not primary).
- Contrast: Celeste starts/stops in ~0.09 s (F2); INSIDE keeps the boy's response immediate and hides the seams with blends (F6).
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: we sit between PoP and INSIDE. The cinematic, rotoscope-like look invites PoP-style commitment, but PoP-style 0.4-0.9 s start/turn delays plus modern players' expectations read as lag ("floaty", "sluggish" — West, F4; Lana critiques in the design reference). Rule: the first visible response is immediate (INSIDE), the full move may ramp (walk 80 -> jog 190 after ~0.5 s of holding is fine because the walk starts at once), and nothing lethal or timed depends on a committed animation. If a committed move exists (vault, crank turn), make it obvious and short, and never place a hazard that punishes its duration.

### F11. Touch latency and touch targets [CONTROLS-FEEL-24][CONTROLS-FEEL-25][CONTROLS-FEEL-26]
- Ng, Annett, Dietz, Gupta & Bischof, "In the Blink of an Eye: Investigating Latency Perception during Stylus Interaction" (CHI 2014, Toronto; DOI 10.1145/2556288.2557037): "the end-to-end latency on most commercial tablets is between 65 and 120 milliseconds" (citing Ng et al. 2012; these are tablets of that era, not today's phones). Prior work they summarise: users could not perceive dragging latency below ~6 ms (range 2-11 ms, Ng et al. 2012); Jota et al. found the JND "while perceiving the 'land-on' portion of the dragging event" at 64 ms (fact-check: the paper ties 64 ms to the land-on of a DRAG, not a tap); Anderson et al. found delays "less than 580 milliseconds were acceptable" for reading/photo/web browsing — noticeable and acceptable are very different bars. Their own stylus result: ~1 vs 2 ms discriminable while dragging, ~7 vs 40 ms while scribbling.
- Fact-check addition (modern phones): Nicosia, Wang, Aschenbrenner et al., "To BYOD or not: Are device latencies important for bring-your-own-device (BYOD) smartphone cognitive testing?" (Behavior Research Methods 55(6):2800-2812, 2023; epub 2022) measured 26 iOS and Android smartphones with a robot (LaTARbot): "total device (sum of display and touch) latencies ranged from 35 to 140 ms". Use 35-140 ms, not 65-120 ms, as the current phone figure.
- Fact-check addition (tension with "act on the press frame"): XAG 107's mobile list says "Control activations should occur on touch-end or mouse-up events" so a mis-touch can be slid off and cancelled, but notes that "activating the function on the down event" is fine where it is "essential" (its examples: playing a piano, shooting a gun). Movement and hold-to-crank are essential down-events; menu, choice and title buttons are not and should fire on release with slide-off cancel. Today Left/Right/USE fire on `button_down` (main.gd:1204-1216); a one-shot gameplay USE can stay on press, but should be a deliberate, documented exception.
- Xbox Accessibility Guideline 107 (Microsoft Learn; updated 2026): suggested minimum touch targets 15 x 15 mm on phones (24 mm on tablets), spaced to avoid accidental presses, with an inactive margin; players should be able to resize/move them and pick preset layouts; avoid or offer alternatives to prolonged holds, rapid taps, multi-finger gestures.
- Apple HIG (Accessibility, Buttons): "a button needs a hit region of at least 44x44 pt" (28x28 pt minimum); "Consider spacing between controls as important as size." The Accessibility page adds numbers (second fact-check): about 12 pt of padding around elements with a bezel and about 24 pt around elements without one.
- Apple HIG "Game controls" (touch guidance added June 10, 2024, updated June 9, 2025) [CONTROLS-FEEL-32], missed in the first pass: frequently used virtual controls at least 44x44 pt, menus at least 28x28 pt; "A virtual control feels unresponsive without a visual and physical press state", so show a press state visible around the finger, plus sound and haptics; keep buttons out of the Home indicator / Dynamic Island safe areas and near the thumb; "For multiple actions, such as walking or sprinting, consider combining the actions into a single control" (supports walk 80 -> jog 190 on one held arrow); movement on the left side, ideally a thumbstick that appears where the thumb lands; hide controls when an action is not available.
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE (derived from `main.tscn`): Left/Right are 96x90 and USE 155x90 in the 960x540 canvas, Left/Right 14 px apart. On a landscape phone ~393 pt tall the canvas scales by ~0.73, so Left/Right are ~70x65 pt (passes Apple's 44 pt) but only ~10-11 mm tall (below XAG's 15 mm suggestion), and the 14 px gap is ~10 pt, below Apple's ~12 pt padding for bezeled controls. Today the touch buttons have no documented press state beyond Godot's default Button look (not checked on screen); Apple's Game controls page makes a visible press state plus sound/haptics a requirement for touch feeling responsive. Phone input already carries ~35-140 ms of device touch+display latency (Nicosia et al. 2023; 65-120 ms on 2012-era tablets per Ng et al. 2014), so the game's own share on phones must be minimal (physics at 60+ Hz, no extra buffering) — and any authored wind-up felt on desktop will feel worse on touch (Playdead, F7).

### F12. Hold-to-act and progress feedback [CONTROLS-FEEL-25][CONTROLS-FEEL-27][CONTROLS-FEEL-28]
- XAG 107's own overview example is our bypass almost exactly: a player required to "hold down the right trigger for 3 seconds to open a door" is excluded if they cannot hold buttons that long (second fact-check addition). It also lists "long press (less than 3 seconds)" as an acceptable alternative to multi-point gestures.
- XAG 107 scopes "hold them for prolonged periods of time (2-3 seconds or more)" as a barrier and cites The Long Dark's "accessible interactions", which "converts all press and hold actions into press actions". Game Accessibility Guidelines (intermediate, motor): "Avoid / provide alternatives to requiring buttons to be held down"; quote from Anthony Martins: "For many hold vs toggle is preference. For disabled gamers is just vital."
- Jakob Nielsen (1993, NN/g): 0.1 s is "the limit for having the user feel that the system is reacting instantaneously"; 1.0 s "the limit for the user's flow of thought to stay uninterrupted"; 10 s the limit for attention; 2-10 s waits want lightweight progress feedback, >10 s a percent-done indicator.
- Code reading (to be confirmed by a rendered/played check, not yet seen on screen): `HoldUse.TICK_SECONDS = 0.4`, the MANUAL BYPASS needs 8 ticks = 3.2 s; the wheel turns (0.18 s tween) and a readout cell lights only when a tick lands, so the first visible wheel response comes ~0.4 s after the press, and a press released before 0.4 s produces no wheel motion at all. Ticks survive release (good: "time, never progress").
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: (a) the press itself must answer within 0.1 s — the engineer's hands grip, the wheel starts creeping with `progress`, a ratchet click — with ticks as the bigger beats; (b) 3.2 s is above XAG's 2-3 s line, so add a "press to crank" (toggle) accessibility option where one press keeps cranking until done or cancelled; (c) keep the in-world cell readout as the progress bar (each tick at 0.4 s is well inside Nielsen's 1 s flow limit); (d) on touch, consider fewer, faster ticks (Playdead, F7).

### F13. Camera: what the research supports for WOLF//OVERRIDE (derived targets; validate by play)
Sources: Keren [1][2], Godot [12][13], Playdead [14], current code. All values below are DERIVED, not shipped measurements.
- Keep: position follow + lerp smoothing (Keren: "most effective and ubiquitous"), fixed zoom during free movement, authored reframes only at beats (agrees with the design reference).
- Add a small camera-window (dead zone) of about +/-36 px (Godot drag margin ~0.1 of the 355 px half-view) so the engineer's 26-29 px auto-steps to crank spots and settle sidesteps never move the camera. Keren: camera-windows exist to avoid small, jarring adjustments.
- Add speed-scaled forward focus (DERIVED design; second fact-check: Keren's Cave Story note is about how FAST the focus slides between its two anchors, "base snapping speed plus walking speed", not about the size of the offset, so the speed-scaled offset is our own choice, not Cave Story's): 0 px when idle, growing to roughly 60-90 px (8-13% of the 711 px view) at jog 190 px/s; ease it in over ~0.5-1 s. Note that smoothing 4.0 already makes the camera trail by ~44 px at 190 px/s, so a 60-90 px target offset nets only ~15-45 px of real lead — tune the pair together. Keren's 25% (Defender/SMB, ~178 px here) is a fast-scroller value; treat it as an upper bound for the Junction sentry beat, where 178 px equals the sentry's 180 px sight range.
- Dual forward-focus threshold: don't swing the look-ahead on a tap-back; reuse the value of the existing `TURN_SETTLE_SECONDS = 0.3` (second fact-check: in m0_actor.gd this constant is WOLF's side-switch settle, not an engineer or camera constant, so give the camera its own constant) (or ~30-40 px of travel) before the focus crosses, mirroring Super Mario World, where the camera "will keep the target point until Mario hits a predefined threshold".
- Cue focus / attractors (Limbo precedent): bias the frame toward the sentry when alerted, toward WOLF when he scouts off ahead, toward the exit when it opens — never during a held interaction.
- Comfort: no zoom or vertical drift during free movement (vestibular mismatch, Keren); shake capped and switchable (already done).
- Verification plan: render.sh stills at 1/4-view intervals on desktop and touch layouts; then hands-on play (camera feel cannot be validated from stills).

### F14. Movement curves for the planned walk 80 / jog 190 (derived from F2, F4, F6, F10)
- Current: velocity snaps to 190 px/s on the press frame and to 0 on release (no curve). Decided: walk 80, jog 190 after ~0.5 s of holding, painted frame-by-frame cycles.
- Targets (DERIVED; validate by play): first visible motion on the press frame (lean/foot lift + footstep onset); 0 -> walk in <= ~0.1 s (Celeste reaches its max in 0.09 s; a heavier human can take a little more but should stay inside Swink's ~100 ms "instant" window); walk -> jog as a 0.2-0.3 s ramp after the ~0.5 s hold, with the cycle cross-faded (INSIDE gets jog "free" by blending); stop from walk in <= ~0.1-0.15 s with a 1-2 frame settle pose; stop from jog may skid up to ~0.2-0.25 s (Celeste-like 0.09 s feels toylike for a human; PoP's ~0.53 s feels committed). Reversal: velocity reverses within the same ~0.1-0.2 s; an authored turn clip plays over it, never gating it (INSIDE: turns get transition clips because blends look silly, not because control waits).
- Playback rate = |velocity| / stride speed so feet never slide (design reference target, agreed).
- Project settings now (project.godot has no physics/V-Sync overrides): physics 60 Hz, V-Sync on with Godot's default swapchain, FPS cap option 30-144. Measure press-to-pixel with a 240 fps phone camera on Mac and phone (West's method) before and after any change; target <= ~67 ms desktop (4 frames) and keep the game's share minimal on phones.

## Where this agrees or disagrees with design-reference-25-games.md
- AGREE (now sourced): "Weight lives in additive layers, never in input latency" (sec. 7.6) — Playdead's own animation slides say direct response is favoured over animation, the button must be pressed "NOW", breathing and look-around are additive layers, and code moves the boy while the animation runs in place [14]. Mick West names animation events as a classic cause of lag [10].
- AGREE: "Zoom changes during free movement: 0" and "reframe only at beats" (sec. 3) — Keren's comfort argument (visual vs vestibular) [1] and region/cinematic-path patterns.
- AGREE + EXTEND: "Shorten timing actions on touch; never auto-complete them" (sec. 6.7) — Playdead's iOS notes give the reason: auto-complete "ends up waiting for triggered animation to end. loses feeling of interaction"; they shortened animations instead, a "Very painful change" [15].
- FILLS GAP: "Turn it Down 90%... only the title was seen" (sec. 6.1) — the deck's notes say effects should "blend into the picture", rain was turned down "till it feels uncomfortable", "effects can't be distracting" [16].
- FILLS GAP: camera look-ahead values (sec. 0, 3) — Keren gives ~25% screen-width forward focus (Defender), Super Mario Bros.' camera-window with a speed-up point ~25% off-center, SMW's threshold-based dual forward-focus, Cave Story's speed-based focus [1]; Godot's smoothing math puts our camera ~44 px BEHIND the engineer at 190 px/s [13]. "No input gives camera look-ahead values" is no longer true.
- FILLS GAP (partly): touch target sizes (sec. 9) — Apple 44x44 pt, XAG 15 mm phone / 24 mm tablet [25][26].
- FILLS GAP: "No reference documents hit-stop, shake or flash values" (sec. 6, 10.7) — Nijman's Nuclear Throne numbers (6 px camera kick, +4 shake, 10-20 ms freeze per hit at 320x240/30 fps) are a real reference [18]; the trauma model is Eiserloh's (GDC 2016) [19]. Our 0.06 s knockdown hit-stop is 3-6x Nijman's per-hit freeze; fine for a rare heavy hit only.
- QUALIFY: "Input to WOLF acknowledgement: Immediate... No source gives seconds" (sec. 2 targets) — sources do give a ceiling for the *system's* first response: ~100 ms (Swink via secondary [7][8]; Nielsen 0.1 s [27]); shipped 60 fps games measure 50-67 ms press-to-screen [9]. WOLF's *decision* can take longer, but an ear flick/head turn should land within ~0.1 s of the request.
- QUALIFY: "Secure action: Hold USE ~1-3 s" (sec. 4 targets) — XAG 107 treats holds of 2-3 s or more as a barrier needing an alternative [25]; keep it <= ~2 s or ship a press-to-toggle option. Our 3.2 s bypass crank is over that line.
- NOTE (arithmetic, not a source): the reference's walk times assume 190 px/s. With the decided walk 80 / jog 190 after ~0.5 s, a held direction still crosses a 1.5-view room (1067 px) in ~5.9 s (0.5 s walking, then jogging), but a player who walks in short presses never reaches jog and needs up to ~13 s. Beat spacing should be checked against both paces once walk/jog is built.

Every quote and number in the first pass's corrected text checks out, except as follows:
- Cave Story [1]: corrected. Keren says Cave Story changes the SNAPPING SPEED between the two forward-focus anchors with walking speed ("base snapping speed plus walking speed"); it is not evidence for an offset whose size scales with speed. The speed-scaled offset stays as a derived design choice.
- West [9]: his figures have the plasma's 2/60 s subtracted (CRT-equivalent), and he says flat panels add ~2 frames. The "<= 67 ms including display" wording in the structured result was wrong; 67 ms is the game's share, and ~100 ms is the realistic end-to-end figure on a flat panel. Also the 30 fps range runs to 233-300 ms, not 167.
- Godot [11]: double-buffered V-Sync (Swapchain Image Count 2) applies only to Forward+ and Mobile; this project renders with gl_compatibility on desktop and mobile, so that lever is not available as configured.
- Apple [26]: added the Accessibility page's ~12 pt (bezeled) / ~24 pt (unbezeled) padding; our ~10 pt Left/Right gap is under it.
- Playdead VFX [16]: the structured result's "Playdead's VFX lead was told" is wrong; Svendsen says he was "still in school" when he joined and the "Turn it down 90%" feedback came from mentors.
- `TURN_SETTLE_SECONDS` (m0_actor.gd:47) is WOLF's side-switch settle; reuse the value, not the constant.
- Pichlmair & Johansen [7]: the "Mario" that caches a press for 1-2 frames is Super Mario Bros. 2 (1988) in their reference list; both it and Braid's 0.23 s come from Fasterholdt, Pichlmair & Holmgård (DiGRA/FDG 2016), not opened.
- Code confirmed: touch buttons 96x90 / 155x90 with a 14 px gap (main.tscn:425-447); camera clamp [355.6, 604.4] and smoothing 4.0 (main.gd:747-758); hit-stops 0.09 / 0.07 / 0.06 s (main.gd:2570, 2696, 2720); the bypass wheel tweens 0.18 s only when a tick lands (bypass_handle.gd:164-172), and a release clears the partial tick (hold_use.gd). Whether the engineer shows a pose or sound on the press frame was not checked on screen.
