## Findings so far (written as read)

### F1. Environmental storytelling = staged space the player interprets [LEVEL-ARCHITECTURE-1][-2][-3]
- GDC 2010, Harvey Smith (Arkane) and Matthias Worch (LucasArts), "What Happened Here? Environmental Storytelling". Vault description (opened): environments with "an inherent sense of history"; tools = props, scripted events, texturing, lighting, scene composition; plus systemic storytelling via "environmental reaction".
- Slide deck (third-party mirror of the 69 slides, opened): definition "Staging player-space with environmental properties that can be interpreted as a meaningful whole, furthering the narrative." Techniques (slide "Practical Techniques"): "Establish a discernible chain of events", "Ensure that event engages the player", "Echo the world at large" (moments draw from the story premise: "Premise spawns events, events remind player of premise"), "Create characterization", "Minimize disconnects between the player's possible actions and prescripted setups." Systemic: "Decals and breakables tell the visual history of combat"; "seeing the site of a battle again triggers the memory of what happened there"; systems that let players see their impact; revisiting spaces. [Fact-check: an earlier draft listed "culturally recognizable objects"; that phrase is not in the mirrored slide text, so it was removed.]
- Smith in Nieman Storyboard (Andrea Pitzer, 2011): "It has to be possible to miss some things to make finding them meaningful." "Every anonymous environmental storytelling moment wastes the opportunity to say something about the game."
- WOLF//OVERRIDE: every room needs one readable "what happened here" chain tied to the premise (the purge, the Director's lockdown, WOLF's escape): e.g. a scorch line from the cage to the shutter, a chair pushed back from the purge terminal, a coffee cup left mid-shift. The Blowback beats (blown door, dropped tank, wrecked sentry) must remain visible on revisit (systemic storytelling = the world remembers). Do not stage things the engineer would obviously want to interact with but can't (Worch's "disconnect").

### F2. Architecture theory for level design (Totten) [LEVEL-ARCHITECTURE-4][-5]
- Totten, An Architectural Approach to Level Design, 2nd ed., A K Peters/CRC Press 2019, 626 pp. ToC (publisher page, opened): ch.5 "Communicating through Environment Art", ch.6 "Building Exciting Levels with Dangerous Architecture", ch.9 "Storytelling in Gamespaces", ch.10 "Possibility Spaces and Worldbuilding". Book content beyond the ToC was NOT read (not available); claims below come from his Game Developer article.
- Totten, "Designing Better Levels Through Human Survival Instincts", Game Developer (Gamasutra), 2011-06-21 (opened): prospect (open, exposed) vs refuge (enclosed, "where early humans could look out into the Prospect spaces of wilderness and evaluate potential threats"); narrow spaces; shade (curiosity) vs shadow (fear); height (frightening at an unprotected edge, but "a very secure feeling when surrounding structure such as walls or railings envelops players"); material qualities shifting from safe and natural to dangerous and industrial (Hero's Journey); the "Secondary Refuge", a refuge seen across prospect space that urges the player forward. "the game level should be the machine for living, dying, and creating tension by exploiting everything in between." [Fact-check: "compression", "release" and "landmark" (as a level device) do not appear in this article; compression and release is attributed to Totten's book, which was not read: UNVERIFIED from his text. Fact-check 2 (2026-10-13): "vertigo" DOES appear: at an unprotected edge "the greatest source of danger is ... the environment itself. This feeling is known as vertigo", and "Vertical elements such as structures or shadows can deepen the sense of vertigo". The first pass wrongly removed it.]
- WOLF//OVERRIDE: the SAFE POINT is a refuge (alcove, low ceiling, warm light, back to a wall, view of where you came from); the Service Junction is prospect (open, exposed to the sentry beam). Corridor -> bulkhead rib (compression) -> catwalk + observation window (release) already in level-expansion.json matches this. A catwalk without a rail reads as vertigo/danger; with rails reads as safe; choose deliberately.

### F3. Door design questions (Liz England, Insomniac) [LEVEL-ARCHITECTURE-6]
- "The Door Problem", lizengland.com, 2014-04-21 (opened). Designer questions (exact): "Can the player open every door in the game?" "How does the player know the difference?" "What tells a player a door is locked and will open, as opposed to a door that they will never open?" "What size is a door? Does it have to be big enough for a player to get through?" "What about allies following you? How many of them need to get through the door without getting stuck?" [Fact-check: her Insomniac affiliation at the time is not stated on the post or her home page (which names System Era, Watch Dogs Legion and Scribblenauts): affiliation UNVERIFIED here.]
- WOLF//OVERRIDE: every door needs an answered card: who uses it (staff, maintenance, vehicles, WOLF), why it is here (connects X to Y), state (open/locked/sealed/broken) and how that state reads without UI (light colour + mechanism position), what is behind it (real background, per Nathanial's 2026-10-11 rule), and whether WOLF can pass. Decorative doors that never open must read as clearly unusable (sealed with a reason: welded, collapsed, "NO ACCESS" plus a physical lock) or should not exist.

### F4. Light and blockmesh guide the player (Naughty Dog / Robert Yang, GDC 2018) [LEVEL-ARCHITECTURE-7][-8][-9]
- GDC 2018 Level Design Workshop "Invisible Intuition: Blockmesh and Lighting Tips to Guide Players and Set the Mood", David Shaver (Naughty Dog) and Robert Yang (NYU Game Center) (Vault page opened).
- Yang's own slides post, Radiator blog 2018-03-31 (opened): "Light for player progression, and highlight exits / critical path"; don't light everything evenly, main entrances brighter than secondary exits (hierarchy); key/fill/rim; rim/background lights to pop silhouettes; "Darker albedos produce much darker rooms than you'd think."
- Shaver interview, 80.lv 2018-05-23 (opened): consistent shape and colour language; "a landmark in the distance"; breadcrumb with interactables; "Put a car or some rubble in the way" instead of invisible walls; "a chain-link fence" blocks while keeping sight; "spiky looking shapes" deter; "add signage during early blockout phases using blocks".
- WOLF//OVERRIDE: the exit of each room is the brightest motivated light in the room's second half; a secondary/blocked door gets less light. Room edges (x=0 and x=width) must be a physical reason to stop (wall, collapsed shutter, fence with view through), never an invisible clamp.

### F5. Real facilities: what a corridor, door and hatch actually measure (US DoD and OSHA) [LEVEL-ARCHITECTURE-10][-11][-12][-13][-14]
MIL-STD-1472H (DoD Design Criteria Standard: Human Engineering, 15 Sep 2020; PDF opened via EverySpec):
- Corridor (fig. 77): one person "non-egress passage forward" 510 mm min / 760 mm preferred; two-person passage 1.22-1.37 m; corridor with one door swinging in 1.67-1.83 m; facing doors 2.13-2.34 m.
- Hinged door (fig. 82; 5.11.7.2.1 says hinged doors "shall have at least the dimensions shown"): 2.0-2.1 m tall, 810-860 mm wide; equipment kept 75 mm out of the door swing; 5.11.7.1.3 entrances placed so people entering "will not inadvertently operate or block access to controls or displays".
- 5.11.7.3.1.1 "Because large doors may jam, an additional hinged door should be provided for personnel entrance and exit." 5.11.7.3.1.2 a hinged door built into a sliding/folding door if it is the only exit.
- 5.11.7.3.3 swinging doors should have windows for visual access; 5.11.7.3.6 glass marked so it is not mistaken for open passage.
- Emergency doors 5.11.7.4.2: "Capable of being opened in 3 seconds or less"; 44-133 N (10-30 lbf); one person out in 5 s; "Simple to locate and operate in the dark." Ceiling along exit >= 2.29 m; protrusions >= 2.04 m.
- Hatches 5.11.7.5: flush with floor/wall where possible; single motion; handle force <= 90 N, overhead <= 220 N; emergency escape hatch min 405x610 mm, preferred 510x710 mm; whole-body access side opening 660-860 mm (fig. 83).
- Stairs (table XXXIV): vertical ladders 75-90 deg; stairs 30-50 deg, preferred 38; personnel ramps 7-15 deg (preferred 7-8); 50-75 deg "inclined stairs" not recommended.
- 5.11.1.9: aisles marked; work and aisle floor space must not overlap; free floor 1.2 m in front of each equipment rack/cabinet. 5.11.2.2 hazardous access panels get an interlock and a label saying so.
- 5.11.6.3.2 guardrail top rail >= 1.1 m (42 in), two rails, toe board >= 150 mm. 5.11.5.2 catwalk floor >= 460 mm. 5.11.6.1.8 exterior platforms open metal grating.
- 5.1.1.2.5 "Rotary valve controls shall open the valve with a counterclockwise motion." 5.1.1.2.6 valves get double-ended arrows labelled at each end (OPEN/CLOSE).
- 5.4.8.2 labels "attached to a structural member that is not removed during equipment servicing"; 5.4.8.6 flat surfaces; 5.4.8.9 non-reflective; 5.4.10.2.1 each cabinet in a work area labelled; 5.4.10.2.3.2 labels on LARGE STORAGE cabinets "should" sit at standing eye height 127-165 cm (50-65 in).
OSHA 29 CFR 1910.36/.37 (opened): exit route ceiling >= 7 ft 6 in (2.3 m); exit access >= 28 in wide; EXIT letters >= 6 in high; exit sign lit to >= 5 fc (54 lux); "Each doorway or passage along an exit access that could be mistaken for an exit must be marked 'Not an Exit' or similar designation, or be identified by a sign indicating its actual use."
OSHA 1910.303(g)(1) (opened): clear working space in front of electrical panels 3-4 ft deep (3 ft at 0-150 V; 3-4 ft at 151-600 V by condition), >= 30 in wide (or the equipment width), headroom 6.5 ft (6.25 ft for installations before Aug 2007); "may not be used for storage"; must be illuminated.
OSHA 1910.144 (opened): red = fire protection, danger, emergency stop; yellow = caution, physical hazards (striking against, tripping, falling, caught in between).
ADA 2010 Standards (access-board.gov, opened): door clear width 32 in min; hardware 34-48 in AFF; reach 15-48 in; wall protrusions between 27 and 80 in limited to 4 in; vertical clearance 80 in.

Converted to WOLF//OVERRIDE world px (engineer 70 px = 1.75 m, so 40 px/m):
- personnel hinged door 2.0-2.1 m = 80-84 px tall, 0.81-0.86 m = 32-34 px clear width (side view shows only the leaf edge / frame). [Fact-check: the earlier "header 100-300 mm = 4-12 px" was a misreading. In fig. 82 the 100-300 mm (4-12 in) dimension is drawn HORIZONTALLY at the top of the hinge side, between the open leaf and the frame (compare 5.11.7.2.3: at least 100 mm between the door at the hinge and a perpendicular wall). It is not a header height. Header depth above a door: no source, UNVERIFIED.]
- planned doors family at 136-144 px = 3.4-3.6 m: that is a vehicle/blast/freight door scale, ~2x the engineer. Fine for the junction blast door or a containment shutter, wrong for the Records west door or any ordinary personnel door. A big door needs a personnel wicket door beside or in it (5.11.7.3.1).
- corridor ceiling >= 2.3 m = 92 px above floor (y <= 348 if floor is 440). Exposed pipes/ducts must clear 2.04 m = 82 px above the walk line on an exit route.
- guardrail 1.1 m = 44 px; toe board 6 px; catwalk grating.
- control/handle height 0.86-1.22 m = 34-49 px above the floor; cabinet label at 1.27-1.65 m = 51-66 px; EXIT sign letters >= 6 px.
- panel clear zone 0.9-1.2 m = 36-48 px in front: a breaker panel must not have props stacked in front of it.
- stairs 30-50 deg (38 preferred); ramps 7-15 deg. The level-expansion plan's "96 px up at 18 degrees" stair is neither: real stairs would be ~38 deg (96 px rise over ~123 px run, about 12 risers of 8 px = 0.2 m each), or a ramp at <=15 deg (>=358 px run). Flag it.

### F6. Airlocks, interlocked doors and controlled entries (real rules) [LEVEL-ARCHITECTURE-17][-18][-20]
- EU GMP Annex 1 (European Commission, C(2022) 5938 final, dated Brussels 22.8.2022, published 2022-08-25; PDF opened), 4.13 (a "should" guideline, not an absolute ban): "the entry and exit doors should not be opened simultaneously"; grade A/B airlocks use an interlocking system; grade C/D "a visual and/or audible warning system ... as a minimum"; "a time delay between the closing and opening of interlocked doors" where needed. 4.14-4.16: positive pressure, >= 10 Pa difference between grades (guidance), pressure indicators fitted between rooms. Glossary: "Airlock - An enclosed space with interlocked doors".
- Federal Select Agent Program BSL-3 inspection checklist based on BMBL 6th ed. (opened): "laboratory has two consecutive self-closing doors."
- SCIF doors (Lori Greene, iDigHardware / Door Security + Safety, 2025-01-16, opened; ICD 705 spec itself blocked, so specifics beyond her article are UNVERIFIED): three perimeter door types (primary entry, secondary, emergency egress-only with no entry); closers without hold-open on the secure side; non-removable hinge pins on outswinging doors; locks to FF-L-2890; egress must still unlatch with one motion, "no key, tool, special knowledge or effort".
- WOLF//OVERRIDE: a containment area, records vault or anything "sealed" should be entered through a two-door vestibule with a status light per door (red = other door open/locked, green = ready), a pressure gauge or "DO NOT OPEN BOTH DOORS" plate, and a short delay. This gives a real reason for a sealed door that the player cannot open yet (the other leaf is open, pressure not equalised, the lockdown holds the interlock) and a believable puzzle (bypass the interlock with the manual crank). Emergency exits in a real secure base open from inside in one motion (OSHA 1910.36(d)(1)); a lockdown that traps people needs a story reason (the Director overriding life safety is itself evidence). Real secure doors have card reader/PIN pad beside the latch side at ~34-48 in (35-49 px) and a closer arm at the top of the leaf.

### F7. Pipes, valves and labels must go somewhere (real marking rules) [LEVEL-ARCHITECTURE-19][-10][-14][-16]
- Michigan State University Construction Standards 220553 (ASME A13.1 based; document footer "Rev. 03/17/2010", posted in the 2018-08 folder; opened): pipe labels "Near each valve and control device", at branches, "Near penetrations through walls, floors, ceilings", at access doors, near equipment "points of origination and termination", every 50 ft (15 m) on runs, 25 ft (7.6 m) in congested areas; labels show service AND a flow arrow; valves get brass tags naming the room/system they control.
- MIL-STD-1472H 5.1.1.2.5-.6: valves open counterclockwise, double-ended OPEN/CLOSE arrow. 5.4.8: labels on structural members, flat, non-reflective.
- OSHA 1910.144/.145: red = fire/danger/stop; yellow = caution/physical hazard (trip, strike, caught-between); danger signs red/black/white; caution yellow background; safety instruction green panel on white; signal word readable at >= 5 ft.
- WOLF//OVERRIDE (screenshot checks): every pipe in the play-plane art starts and ends at a visible thing (a wall penetration with a collar, a pump, a tank, a valve, the relay tower) or leaves frame; none ends in air. Pipes that matter to a beat (the coolant line in Blowback) carry a flow arrow and a stencilled service name near the valve and at the wall penetration, so the overload route is readable before the player acts. Yellow/black hazard striping only on real physical hazards (the blast-door swing zone, the tank drop zone, floor edges), never as decoration (currently striped pipe in junction capture: only correct if that pipe is a hazard). Valve wheel art turns counterclockwise to open.
- At 40 px/m, 15 m = 600 px: in a 1440-1680 px room each long pipe run carries 2-3 labels, one at each penetration.

### F8. How real people and studios make a space read as lived-in and logical [LEVEL-ARCHITECTURE-21][-24][-25][-28][-26]
- Arkane, Prey (Game Informer, Jeff Cork, 2016-12-09, opened). Ricardo Bare: "it doesn't make any sense that someone has to walk this far to do this." "this place doesn't have a bathroom. Where do these people pee?" "We know where everybody's sleeping quarters are, where they eat, where they work." Security officers carry handguns or tasers, not a bazooka (equipment fits the staff).
- Arkane, Dishonored 2 (Inverse, Benjamin Kratsch, 2016-11-04, opened). Sebastien Mitton: "You don't have to study design to enter a room in a game and you automatically know if there is something off." Geography carries story (wind through the gaps powers the city).
- Creative Assembly, Alien: Isolation (MCV, 2014-02-13, opened): for kit-bashed props, "Creative Assembly limited themselves to only using objects that were made before 1979" [Fact-check: corrected from "nothing that could not have existed on the 1979 set", which overstates the article]; Fox supplied "three terabytes of behind the scenes photos, videos and assets"; sets rebuilt at 1:1 scale from blueprints, then panels/doors/furniture extracted into a style guide; kit-bashed props from real household objects.
- Valve, Half-Life: Alyx (Road to VR, Ben Lang, 2020-04-14, opened). Robin Walker: built "one 'room' at a time"; in the quarantine zone "players explored more, so we put more art in it, and then they explored more"; in a typical Half-Life 2 room "maybe 75% or 80% of players run through the room" and "5 to 10% of the players explore one thing and another 5% of players explore another thing", while in VR "everyone looks at the vent, everyone pokes their head under the desk." [Fact-check: reworded from "only 5-10% found hidden details"; Walker's figure is about players stopping to examine a room's props, an estimate, not a measured statistic.]
- Eric Chahi, Another World (Game Developer, Christian Nutt, 2011-03-03, GDC 2011 classic postmortem, opened): "The environment suggests a narrative and gives a meaning to these more basic actions." "The real media is the player's memory and imagination." Solo; by December 1989, after 17 months, one third was done [Fact-check: corrected from "~19 months"].
- WOLF//OVERRIDE: write a one-paragraph "facility brief" per room before painting: what the room is for in the base's operation, who worked here on a normal day (shift engineers, records clerks, maintenance drones), how they got in (personnel door, freight lift, service tunnel), what services pass through (power, coolant, data, air), what changed since the lockdown. Every object in the plate must trace to that brief (Alien: Isolation's 1979 rule => "nothing that the base's own engineers would not have installed"). Equipment types match staff: a maintenance corridor has tool lockers, a fire extinguisher, an eyewash/first-aid point, cable trays; a records room has racks, a clerk's desk and chair, a document shredder or purge terminal; a machine-run base still has human-scale fixtures (handrails, light switches, signage) because humans maintain it.

### F9. Repetition and kits [LEVEL-ARCHITECTURE-22][-30]
- Joel Burgess (Bethesda), "Skyrim's Modular Approach to Level Design", Game Developer blog 2013-05-01 (GDC 2013 with Nate Purkeypile), opened: 7 kits made ~400+ dungeon cells in 2.5 years; 128 units = one character height; rooms 512x512x512, halls 256; players notice "repeated clutter first, then the repeated architecture"; mix kits; "Lighting and rendering features like ambient occlusion ... go a long way to hide any awkward intersections."
- Inigo Quilez, "Texture Repetition", iquilezles.org 2015 (opened): "One of the most typical issues of using textures to cover large surfaces is the visible repetition"; random offset/orientation per tile with blending (4 fetches), Voronoi (9), virtual-pattern blend (2).
- WOLF//OVERRIDE: rooms are unique painted plates, so architecture repetition is low; the risk is repeated PROPS (the same breaker cabinet as "BREAKER", "OVERLOAD BREAKER" and "TANK RELEASE" in the junction capture) and repeated wall-light/beacon spacing. Rule: never show the same prop sprite twice in one view unless the room's brief says it is a bank of identical units (and then 3+ in a regular row, as real switchgear is). Vary wear per instance. The wet-floor reflection treatment should not repeat the same puddle pattern across rooms.

### F10. Contact and grounding: why floating props read as fake [LEVEL-ARCHITECTURE-29]
- Kersten, Mamassian & Knill (1997) "Moving cast shadows induce apparent motion in depth", Perception 26; lab demo page (opened): "When the shadow is diagonal, the ball appears to slide along the floor ... When the shadow trajectory is horizontal, the ball appears to rise above the floor of the box." The shadow, not the object, tells the eye where the object sits. [Fact-check: the study is about MOVING shadows and apparent motion in depth (Perception 26(2):171-192). Applying it to static props (contact shadow at 0 px gap = "on the floor") is a reasonable design extrapolation, not something the paper measured.]
- WOLF//OVERRIDE: every object on the floor needs a contact shadow/AO band touching its base (0 px gap) and a base that meets the painted floor line; every wall-mounted object needs a backplate, brackets/bolts and its service connection (conduit, pipe, cable) running to a wall/ceiling; every hung object needs a hanger traced to structure (hoist rail, chain to a beam). A prop whose shadow is offset or missing reads as hovering, matching Nathanial's "controllers and computers in the air" complaint.

## Screenshot checklist for a level artist (check each against a 1280x720 render; world px at 40 px/m, engineer = 70 px)
Doors
1. Every door sits in a wall or bulkhead that visibly continues above it to the ceiling and below it to the floor; no free-standing door frames. A door across the walk line shows the next space through/around it when open; a back-wall door has a real space (passage, duct, lit room) painted behind its opening. [England; Nathanial 2026-10-11 rule]
2. Personnel door leaf 80-98 px tall (real 80-84 px); freight/blast doors (>= 136 px) carry a brief reason AND a personnel wicket door or adjacent hinged door. [MIL-STD fig. 82, 5.11.7.3.1]
3. Every door shows its state without UI: status light (red/amber/green) by the latch side, mechanism position (bolts, drum, leaf), and for locked doors a physical lock/plate; a door that never opens is visibly sealed with a reason (welded, chained, "NOT AN EXIT"/"NO ACCESS", collapsed) or is cut. [OSHA 1910.37(b)(5); England]
4. Controls for a door (reader, PIN pad, crank, release) sit on the latch side at 35-49 px above the floor, mounted on the wall/frame with a conduit, not floating. [ADA 404.2.7, MIL-STD]
5. Equipment is not placed in a door's swing or in front of a doorway (75 mm = 3 px clear minimum, in practice keep a door-width clear). [MIL-STD 5.11.7.2.4, 5.11.7.1.3]
6. Sealed/containment areas use two doors with an interlock cue (both status lights, pressure gauge, "DO NOT OPEN BOTH DOORS"). [Annex 1 4.13; BMBL BSL-3]
Grounding
7. Every floor object: base on the floor line, contact shadow touching the base, 0 px gap. [Kersten 1997; design-reference §7]
8. Every wall object: backplate + fixings + a service line (conduit/pipe/cable) to wall or ceiling (design inference: no standard opened says this for props; MIL-STD 5.4.8 is about mounting LABELS on structure); mounting height plausible (controls 15-49 px by ADA 308 reach, door hardware 35-49 px by ADA 404.2.7, storage-cabinet labels 51-66 px, overhead signs above 82 px). [MIL-STD 5.4.8, 5.4.10.2.3.2; ADA 308, 404.2.7]
9. Every hung object traces to structure (hoist beam, chain to a beam, cable tray hanger).
10. Every pipe starts and ends at something (penetration collar, pump, tank, valve, equipment) or leaves the frame; beat-relevant pipes carry a flow arrow and service stencil near valves and penetrations. [MSU 220553 / ASME A13.1]
Scale
11. Ceiling of a walkway >= 92 px above the floor; pipes/ducts over a walk line clear >= 82 px. [OSHA 1910.36(g)(1); MIL-STD 5.11.7.4.3-4]
12. Handrails on catwalks/platforms at 44 px with a mid rail and a 6 px toe board; catwalk deck >= 18 px deep in plan (in side view: visible grating). [MIL-STD 5.11.6.3.2]
13. Stairs between 30 and 50 deg (38 preferred) with equal risers about 7-8 px; ramps 7-15 deg; no 16-29 deg "stairs". [MIL-STD table XXXIV]
14. Two copies of the same prop sprite never appear in one view unless they are an intentional row of 3+ identical units. [Burgess]
Logic and story
15. The room has a one-paragraph brief (purpose, staff, services, access, what changed); every object traces to it; nothing contradicts it (no bazooka in a records room). [Bare/Prey; Alien: Isolation 1979 rule]
16. The room's exit is the brightest motivated light in its half of the room and is visible (or its landmark is) from the entrance; non-exit doors are darker. [Yang 2018; Shaver]
17. Room edges are physical reasons to stop (wall, shutter, rubble, fence with view through), not an invisible clamp. [Shaver]
18. One "what happened here" chain per room readable without text; it can be missed. [Smith & Worch 2010; Smith 2011]
19. Hazard striping (yellow/black) only on real hazards; red reserved for fire/danger/stop; green for safety (safe point, first aid, eyewash). [OSHA 1910.144/.145]
20. Labels identifying equipment are physical plates on the object (stencil, engraved plate); floating world-space names appear only as an in-range prompt. [MIL-STD 5.4.8; design-reference §7 principle 4]

## Agree / disagree with design-reference-25-games.md
Agree:
- §7.3 "Ground everything" and target "Contact shadows 100%": supported by Kersten et al. 1997 (shadow position sets perceived height). Extend from characters to every prop, and add wall-mount and hang rules.
- §3.10 safe points at thresholds: Totten's refuge (alcove, back to wall, view out) gives the architectural form; MIL-STD/OSHA put lit exits and doors at thresholds anyway.
- §3 Next: "the room's goal shown as a fog-cutting light visible from the entrance": Yang 2018 "highlight exits / critical path"; Shaver landmark in the distance.
- §3.7 two approaches per room (engineer route, WOLF route): agree, but WOLF's route must be a real service structure sized for a 115x61 px body (see F11), not a vent.
- §7.4 UI only when actionable: agree; add that permanent station names should become physical plates (MIL-STD 5.4.8).
Disagree or refine:
- §10.6 "Width supported only if every view-width carries a beat": refine. A beat can be an environmental story vignette or a readable service element, not only a gameplay beat (Smith & Worch 2010; Walker 2020: more art -> more exploration). Empty floor is the problem, not the absence of an interaction.
- Not addressed by the 25-games study: scale. Doors planned at 136-144 px (~2x the 70 px engineer) are freight/blast scale; real personnel doors would be 80-84 px; game conventions run 1.25-1.56x player height (Level Design Book table). Pick per-door by its brief.
- Not addressed: stairs. level-expansion.json's "96 px up at 18 degrees" stair is neither a stair (30-50 deg) nor a personnel ramp (7-15 deg) by MIL-STD-1472H table XXXIV.
- Gap filled partly: flip-screen to scrolling (Oddworld blog: AI, level design and puzzles had to be adapted); the 3-screen cap stays UNVERIFIED.
