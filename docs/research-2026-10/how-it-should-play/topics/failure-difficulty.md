## Findings

### F1. Flow is a balance plus a sense of control; let the player steer difficulty through play [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-1]
- Chen's MFA thesis (2006) restates Csikszentmihalyi's eight flow components (challenge needing skill, merging of action and awareness, clear goals, direct feedback, concentration, sense of control, loss of self-consciousness, transformed time) and reduces them to three a game must have: intrinsic reward, challenge matched to ability, and "a sense of personal control".
- Different players have different flow zones ("like fingerprints"); a game tuned for normal players misses novice and hardcore ones.
- He argues passive (system-driven) DDA has four unsolved problems: no direct data, "Performance does not mirror Flow", assumptions (a death count may be a stunt), and rigid designer-set changes. His alternative: active DDA, choices embedded in core play so players "subconsciously" pick their challenge.
- Numbers: Traffic Light prototype: choice-based DDA stretched a 1-2 minute timing game to about 5-12 minutes, but asking the player between every round "broke the player's Flow". flOw: 20 levels; death pushes you back one level ("minimal death penalty"); >350,000 downloads in the first two weeks (Flash version).
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: the approved "engineer-only route is slower and costlier, WOLF's help is faster" structure IS Chen's embedded active DDA: the player's choice (ask WOLF / crank it yourself, which bypass route, when to drop the tank) sets the difficulty without a menu. Do NOT add a popup "make this easier?" after every death (Traffic Light shows frequent explicit DDA choices break flow). Death should cost little in place (flOw's push-back-one-step).

### F2. Failing some, and feeling responsible for it, makes players enjoy the game MORE [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-2]
- Juul, "Fear of Failing?" (2009): 85 online players of a Pac-Man/Snake hybrid prototype (73 of 85 male, mostly avid players: not a general audience). "The most positive players were the ones that failed some, and then completed the game"; completing without failing got lower ratings. CAVEAT (fact-check): this performance result was only "close to statistical significance (p=0.06)". The significant result (p<0.016) compares players who answered "The game was too hard" with those who answered "I made a mistake": the self-blaming group rated the game higher. Of players calling games "too easy", 36% cited lack of challenge, 27% not having to rethink strategy, 6% never failing (5% not being measured on performance).
- Key line (verbatim, checked): "failing, and feeling responsible for failing, makes players enjoy a game more, not less."
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: zero-death design is not the goal; the goal is deaths the player attributes to themselves. Every knockdown/death needs a visible cause the player could have read (warning before danger), so the attribution is "I stepped in the vent", not "the sentry cheated". The approved death card that names cause and fix (hazard.gd "the cause and the fix") is exactly right.

### F3. Classic DDA (Hamlet): detect "flailing", then intervene quietly, iteratively, leaving room for trial and error [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-3]
- Hunicke and Chapman, "AI for Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment in Games" (Northwestern, 2004; Hamlet on the Half-Life engine). Flailing = "repeatedly edging towards a state where her current means can no longer accomplish necessary and immediate ends". CORRECTION (fact-check): Hamlet's primary assessment signal is inventory/health shortfall (damage over time, "repeated inventory shortfalls"), not deaths. Deaths (at this location, in the level, in the game), the player's location and progress, how often the encounter was repeated and how many interventions so far are the observations that set the COST of each intervention. Its worked example does define flailing as "repeatedly dying before completing the engagement". Venue confirmed on Hunicke's own CV: Proceedings of the Challenges in Game AI Workshop, Nineteenth National Conference on Artificial Intelligence (AAAI 2004).
- Numbers: "Comfort Zone" policy keeps the player around 50% health (dips to 25%, crests to 75%); "Discomfort Zone" keeps experienced players fighting back from 15-20% health. Enemies are tuned "only if they repeatedly overwhelm the player". Interventions: donate a health pack, reduce enemy accuracy, etc. Warns that interventions that ignore the player's perception may "interrupt their overall suspension of disbelief".
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: there is no health or ammo economy, so the equivalent levers are time windows (tank-drop window, sentry LOCK_SECONDS, valve cranks), WOLF's hint lines and WOLF's willingness. Use per-location death counts (Hamlet's main signal) to unlock in-fiction help after repeated deaths at the same beat. Keep it invisible and in-fiction, never a menu.

### F4. Hidden DDA in a shipped action platformer: Crash Bandicoot (Naughty Dog, 1996 onward) [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-4]
- Andy Gavin's "Making Crash Bandicoot - part 6" (2011; the passage is written by Jason Rubin per the page): if a novice "died a lot of times", they got an Aku Aku mask (one free hit) at the start of a round; dying a lot running from the boulder slowed the boulder "a little each time"; "If you died too much a fruit crate would suddenly become a continue point." Players "never realized" they were "all playing a slightly different game". FACT-CHECK 2: the passage does not date each trick to the 1996 game ("We were already learning"; the "never realized" line is about the later Crash games it "would lead" to), and Crash is a mostly into-the-screen 3D platformer, not a side-view game. Rubin in the same passage calls the first game's "annoying way of making players earn continues" "a major mistake": "It makes players that need lives fail while boring players that don't." The team's mantra: "help weaker players without changing the game for the better players".
- Numbers: no death thresholds are given on the page (UNVERIFIED how many deaths).
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: the closest precedent for a 2D-ish cinematic action beat. Three cheap, invisible levers map directly: (a) a nearer restore point after N deaths at one beat (the "crate becomes a checkpoint" move), (b) widen the one timing window that killed you (the "slow the boulder" move: tank-drop window, sentry LOCK_SECONDS, cloud length), (c) a one-shot protection equivalent (a steam knockdown instead of a kill on the next contact). Never move the goalposts on the first death.

### F5. Left 4 Dead's AI Director adjusts PACING, not difficulty [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-5]
- Michael Booth (Valve), "The AI Systems of Left 4 Dead" (AIIDE 2009 talk slides). Survivor Intensity rises with damage taken, incapacitation, being pulled off a ledge, nearby kills; it decays toward zero over time but not while being engaged.
- Phases and numbers: Build Up (full threat until intensity peaks) -> Sustain Peak "for 3-5 seconds" -> Peak Fade (minimal threats until intensity decays) -> Relax: "Maintain minimal threat population for 30-45 seconds, or until Survivors have traveled far enough toward the next safe room". CORRECTION (fact-check): the 90-180 s figure is for Mob spawns ("Occur at randomized interval between 90 and 180 seconds"), not "special events". "Boss Encounters NOT affected by adaptive pacing". Key line: "Algorithm adjusts pacing, not difficulty" ("Amplitude (difficulty) is not changed, frequency (pacing) is").
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: after a death or a big action peak (blowback, sentry chase), guarantee a relax window before the next threat. The 30-45 s relax figure is a usable upper bound for a quiet stretch between Junction beats; WOLF scenes, records and safe points belong in those valleys. Keep authored big beats (the tank drop, the exit-bolt overload) unmodulated, like L4D's bosses.

### F6. Shipped assist that scales with failure: Hades God Mode (Supergiant, 2020) [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-6] [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-6B]
- CORRECTED in fact-check 2: the primary interview is Inverse (Tomas Franzese, 11 Aug 2021, "'Hades' devs reveal how God Mode solves the worst thing about the genre"). The Can I Play That? piece (Ben Bayliss, 11 Aug 2021, actual title "Hades God Mode origins explained by Supergiant Games") is a news summary of that Inverse interview ("Kasavin told Inverse"), not its own interview. Inverse: God Mode's boon "immediately makes the player take 20 percent less damage", which "increases by two percent after each death, ultimately capping off at 80 percent resistance" (the 80% cap is now VERIFIED in the primary). First added in a December 2019 early access update; it can be toggled on and off at any time in settings. Kasavin: "Our big focus from the start with Hades was how to take the sting of failure and reduce that as much as possible". CAVEAT for a linear game: Kasavin says "I don't know that God Mode is transferable to other games because it's designed so specifically around the structure of Hades", and Inverse adds that it "wouldn't be as meaningful in a more linear game like Uncharted". WOLF//OVERRIDE is linear, so a per-death scaling assist stays a COULD, not a model to copy. Kasavin: "the way to approach difficulty settings may need to be proprietary to the game"; and "I don't underestimate the extraordinary amount of work it takes to implement a thoughtfully created easy mode" (quote corrected in fact-check; the earlier version dropped "a thoughtfully created").
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: an opt-in assist that grows with each death is a good fit for a story-led game: it never takes control away, it is the player's choice (Chen's control), and it fits the fiction if framed as WOLF/engineer learning the room. Make it opt-in and changeable any time, no shaming name.

### F7. Platform guidance on difficulty: XAG 108 (Microsoft) [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-7]
- XAG 108 "Game difficulty options": "ideally four or more" difficulty presets; "change difficulty at any time without losing game progress"; regular save with "both manual and auto-save options" "so that players can continue after failure without significant loss of progress"; discrete per-mechanic difficulty or assists; "an ultra-low difficulty mode ... to progress through the narrative"; a mode completable with "a greatly reduced control scheme"; preset names must not denigrate the player ("Wimp Mode"); single-player games "pausable at any time", including cutscenes.
- Example numbers in XAG 108: Grounded autosave interval adjustable in 1-minute steps up to 20 minutes; Ori: 4 presets; Forza Horizon 4: 8 Drivatar levels.
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: the slice has no difficulty options today. Minimum: pause anywhere including the death card and cutscenes (check), autosave per beat (decided), and a per-mechanic assist set (timing windows, hazard damage, hold-to-crank) rather than one Easy/Normal/Hard switch, because the game's challenges are few and distinct (timing, reading a hazard, holding). A "Story" preset that keeps every beat but cannot kill (knockdown only) is the ultra-low mode.

### F8. Holds, mashing, QTEs and touch: XAG 107 and the Game Accessibility Guidelines [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-8]
- XAG 107 "Input": scoping question flags any place a player "must press buttons quickly or hold them for prolonged periods of time (2-3 seconds or more)"; "Avoid introducing mechanics where a key or button should be held down for an extended period before the input is registered"; a "long press (less than 3 seconds)" is listed as an acceptable ALTERNATIVE to multi-point gestures (context checked: it is not a general hold-length rule); offer toggles / "auto" holds; Gears 5's "Button Tap Challenges" option lets players choose "press & hold" instead of quick taps; The Long Dark's "accessible interactions" "converts all press and hold actions into press actions". Mobile: touch targets minimum 15 x 15 mm on phones (118 x 118 px at 200 DPI), 24 x 24 mm on tablets; activation on touch-end; avoid prolonged holds, rapid taps, multi-finger gestures; let players resize/move targets.
- Game Accessibility Guidelines (Ian Hamilton et al.): Basic: "Include an option to adjust the game speed"; "Offer a wide choice of difficulty levels"; "Ensure no essential information is conveyed by sounds alone" and "by a fixed colour alone"; "Ensure interactive elements / virtual controls are large and well spaced". Intermediate: "Allow difficulty level to be altered during gameplay, either through settings or adaptive difficulty"; "Avoid / provide alternatives to requiring buttons to be held down"; "Avoid repeated inputs (button-mashing/quick time events)"; "Provide an autosave feature"; "Offer a means to bypass gameplay elements that aren't part of the core mechanic". Advanced: "Do not make precise timing essential to gameplay - offer alternatives, actions that can be carried out while paused, or a skip mechanism".
- GAG levels checked on the full list (fact-check): "Include an option to adjust the game speed" and "Offer a wide choice of difficulty levels" are Basic; also Basic: "Avoid flickering images and repetitive patterns" (ties to F21).
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: the approved safe-point "short committed hold" (~1-3 s in the study) and the MANUAL BYPASS (measured in code: 8 ticks x 0.4 s = 3.2 s of held USE) both sit at or above XAG's 2-3 s flag. Required: a "Hold actions: hold / toggle (tap to start, tap to stop)" option; hold_use.gd already keeps ticks across a release, which is good (progress survives a lift), so a toggle is cheap. Safe-point hold should be <=1.5 s by default (under the XAG 2-3 s flag). Sentry warning must be visual AND audio; the tank-drop timing must have a non-timing alternative or a widen-window assist (Advanced guideline). Touch: the USE button >=15 mm.

### F9. Celeste Assist Mode: the framing is part of the design [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-9]
- Vice/Waypoint (Patrick Klepek, "Why The Very Hard 'Celeste' is Perfectly Fine With You Breaking Its Rules", 7 Feb 2018): options change "game speed, stamina, the number of air dashes, even your ability to die". In-game pitch: "Celeste was designed to be a challenging, but accessible game. We believe that its difficulty is essential to the experience." Thorson: "We want people to come out of this game feeling capable and powerful, so that means we have to teach them, challenge them, and support them through the failures along the way." Assist Mode was first called "Cheat Mode", renamed because it felt "judgmental".
- Numbers: game speed 50, 60, 70, 80, 90 or 100%, changeable from the pause screen at any time (fact-check 2: confirmed by AbleGamers' Accessible Player Experiences "Slow It Down" pattern [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-35], an accessibility charity's documented example; still not a developer-primary source). Official Player.cs (Noel Berry's public source): JumpGraceTime = 0.1 s (coyote time), DashCornerCorrection = 4 px, DashCooldown = 0.2 s, ClimbMaxStamina = 110; Die() calls Leader.LoseFollowers() and increments session.Deaths and DeathsInCurrentLevel (a per-room death counter exists in the shipped game).
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: name the assist screen without judgement ("Assist", never "Easy/Cheat"); default off, explained once; game-speed slider (50-100% in 10% steps) is the single most general assist for every timing beat in the Junction (tank drop, sentry, cloud). Add hidden forgiveness by default the way Player.cs does: ~0.1 s grace on the edges of every timing window, and a few pixels of correction on hazard edges, so near-misses go the player's way.

### F10. Players' sense of fairness is psychological, not mathematical (Sid Meier, GDC 2010) [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-10]
- Game Developer (Kris Graft, 12 Mar 2010), on Meier's GDC 2010 keynote ("The Psychology of Game Design" per Game Informer). CORRECTION (fact-check): "while gamers are happy to take credit for success, if they lose, the blame often falls on the game" is Graft's paraphrase, not a Meier quote. Meier's own words: "Whenever something random happens to the player, paranoia sets in"; "You almost cannot reward the player enough" in the first 15 minutes of a game. Game Informer (byline "Game Informer Editorial", 12 Mar 2010): testers were as happy winning at 3-to-1 as at 1-to-3, but losing at 20-to-10 felt wrong ("It's 20-to-10! I should win!"), although it equals 2-to-1.
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: no hidden dice in lethal beats. The sentry and hazards must be deterministic and readable so every loss is attributable (ties to Juul F2). If any randomness exists (sentry patrol timing, cloud drift), bias it toward the player and never randomise the lethal edge.

### F11. How many players get how far: Steam global achievement rates (measured, owners-based) [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-11]
- Celeste (Steam app 504230): first-chapter achievement "Forsaken" 70.7% (Steam hides this achievement's description; "chapter 1" is inferred from the chapter name Forsaken City); "Climb Celeste Mountain" (main story) 34.6%; Farewell 8.8%.
- Hollow Knight (367520): "Defeat the False Knight" (first boss) 71.3%; "Defeat Hornet in Greenpath" 62.7%; The Hollow Knight ending 24.5%; Steel Soul (permadeath) "Finish the game in Steel Soul mode" 4.7%.
- Alien: Isolation (214490): mission 1 89.3%, mission 2 68.5%, "Encounter the Alien in Sevastopol for the first time" 58.1%, mission 3 52.3%, mission 4 41.9%, mission 5 33.8%; "Complete the game on any difficulty setting" 20.1%; hardest difficulty 8.1%; "Navigate Reactor Maintenance without dying" 6.9%.
- Limbo (48000): "No Point in Dying" ("Complete the game in one sitting with five or less deaths") 2.5%.
- Caveat: percentages are of Steam accounts that own the game (including people who never launched it or bought it in bundles), read 2026-10-12; they are upper-level funnel shapes, not cause analysis.
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: the steepest losses in every game are EARLY (Alien loses ~21 points between missions 1 and 2 and ~16 more by mission 3, i.e. around when the alien starts hunting; Celeste loses ~30% before finishing chapter 1). The first slice IS the early game: the corridor and the Junction must be the fairest, best-signposted deaths in the game. The Limbo 2.5% shows near-deathless play is rare even in a short, readable, forgiving game: plan for multiple deaths per beat, not zero.

### F12. Csikszentmihalyi's flow, via Chen and Sweetser & Wyeth [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-12]
- Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience (Harper & Row, 1990) is the primary book; Chen's thesis restates its eight components and its challenge/skill channel: challenge above ability gives anxiety, below gives boredom, with "a fuzzy safe zone" between (Chen paraphrasing Csikszentmihalyi 1990). Chen's flowtheory page: "Not all of these components need to be present together for Flow to be experienced."
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: clear goals and direct feedback are the cheapest flow components and the most at risk in a cinematic game with no HUD: the player must always know the current goal (door, valve, relay) and get instant feedback on each input (study's "instant acknowledgement"). Death breaks flow; the shorter and quieter the death-to-control gap, the less flow is lost.

### F13. Easier kept players longer; short time limits were the most disengaging factor (Lomas et al., CHI 2013) [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-13]
- Lomas, Patel, Forlizzi, Koedinger, "Optimizing Challenge in an Educational Game Using Large-Scale Design Experiments" (CHI 2013). Two online experiments, 10K and 70K subjects (experiment 1: 10,478 sessions). "subjects were more engaged and played longer when the game was easier, which seems to contradict the generality of the Inverted-U Hypothesis." Largest targets: 63% success vs 29% for the smallest; easier targets gave 34% more estimates and 18% more time played. "Longer time limits increased both success and engagement"; "the shortest time limits were the most disengaging of any design factor." Caveat from the authors: even the easiest condition had <70% success, so the very-easy end was not tested; this is an educational math game, not an action game.
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: a counterweight to "make it hard for flow". For a story-first game, err easier, especially on time pressure: the tank-drop window (~0.63 s patrol / ~0.38 s chase in the study) and any countdown are the riskiest parts of the slice. Lomas plus Juul together: some failure is good, but tight time windows cost players fastest.

### F14. "Remove lives, reduce respawn time, keep the levels short and keep the goal always in sight" (Super Meat Boy, Team Meat) [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-14]
- Edmund McMillen, Game Developer (2010): "The time it takes for Meat Boy to die and respawn is almost instantaneous"; removing lives puts the focus "more on the actual level design and challenge and less around the penalty of losing lives". The clear-replay shows "the player's past 40-plus attempts all playing at once". Team Meat postmortem (Edmund McMillen and Tommy Refenes; Game Developer Magazine postmortem reprinted on Gamasutra, byline Refenes, 14 Apr 2011): "Remove lives, reduce respawn time, keep the levels short and keep the goal always in sight." McMillen 2010 also: the player "doesn't even have time to think about dying before being placed right back where he left off."
- Fact-check 2 addition: McMillen's 2010 article frames difficulty as a formula, "(Percent chance the player will die) X (Penalty for dying) = Difficulty", and says that once lives are removed "the penalty for death basically turned into the amount of time you took to restart". That is the arithmetic behind keeping the replay short: a hard beat stays fair if the penalty term is a second or two.
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: "goal always in sight" is the new piece: after a death in the Junction the retry framing should show the target (the valve, the drop mark, the door) on the first frame of control. The respawn point should face the challenge, not a corridor before it.

### F15. Hard games say difficulty is a means to accomplishment, and keep failure costly on purpose (Miyazaki) [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-15]
- Phil Kollar, Game Informer (2009), interviewing Hidetaka Miyazaki on Demon's Souls: "Having the game be 'difficult' was never the goal. What we set out to do was strictly to provide a sense of accomplishment." Losing souls on death "was necessary to give players that sense of accomplishment"; "if the Souls could be recovered anytime, there would be no suspense".
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: supports the decided "Secure the Evidence" drop as a source of suspense, but WOLF//OVERRIDE is a story game whose audience is not self-selected for Souls difficulty. Keep the Souls cost only on OPTIONAL finds (decided), never on essential progress. Steam data (F11) shows even Hollow Knight loses ~29% before its first boss.

### F16. Alien: Isolation added Novice two months after launch [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-16]
- Engadget (Sinan Kubba, 9 Dec 2014): a free update added Novice ("a slower, less adaptive and more attention-deficit xenomorph that sounds much easier to hide from", less demanding combat including androids, more resources) and Nightmare (an "ultimate alien" that is "more aggressive and adapts quicker", an "unreliable" tracker, scarcer flamethrower fuel). CORRECTION (fact-check): the game already shipped with Easy, Normal and Hard ("you can already choose from Easy, Normal and Hard modes"); Novice was an extra, lower tier added later. The October 2014 launch date (about two months earlier) is not stated in the opened article; fact-check 2 confirmed it from Steam's store data (release date "Oct 6, 2014" [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-37]), so Novice came about 9 weeks after launch. Steam (F11): 20.1% finished on any difficulty; 8.1% on "the hardest difficulty setting".
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: a tense hunter (the sentry) needs its lowest tier at launch, not as a patch (Alien had an Easy mode at launch and still needed a lower one). The levers Alien chose are exactly the readable ones: hunter speed, how fast it adapts, and how easy hiding is. For the sentry: CHASE_SPEED (230 > the engineer's 190 walk), LOCK_SECONDS (0.5), LOSE_SECONDS (2.5), SIGHT_RANGE (180).

### F17. Platform touch and resume guidance (Apple HIG) [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-17]
- Apple Human Interface Guidelines, Game controls: "Make sure frequently used controls are a minimum size of 44x44 pt, and less important controls, such as menus, are a minimum size of 28x28 pt"; "Always include visible and tactile press states." Designing for games: with GameSave, help people "start back up exactly where they left off on a different device" (CORRECTION, fact-check: this is about cloud saves across devices, not interruptions); onboarding as "a playable tutorial that engages people quickly and helps them feel successful right away". Minimum text size iOS 11 pt (default 17 pt).
- ADDED in fact-check [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-34] Apple HIG, Multitasking: "your app or game always needs to be prepared to save and restore their context"; "Pause activities that require people's attention or active participation when they switch away. If your app is a game ... make sure people don't miss anything when they switch to another app. When they switch back, let them continue as if they never left." It also names "an incoming phone call" as an audio interruption to handle. This is the correct primary source for the phone-interruption need: auto-pause in place on backgrounding; the last autosave is only the fallback if the OS ends the app.
- XAG 107 asks for more on phones: 15 x 15 mm. At a typical ~460 ppi phone this is far above 44 pt (44 pt is about 7 mm). Treat 44 pt as the floor and aim larger for USE and the retry button.
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: the death card's RETRY and USE skip must be >=44 pt touch targets with a press state; on backgrounding or a phone call mid-beat, the game pauses in place (main.gd already does this on APPLICATION_PAUSED / FOCUS_OUT, fact-check 2) and, if the OS ends the app, resumes at the last autosave with nothing lost that was secured (the study's "later" item becomes a first-slice need because mobile is a first-slice target).

### F18. Celeste's forgiveness, in Thorson's words (via Gigazine's report of the 2020 thread) [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-18]
- Gigazine (17 Mar 2020) reporting Maddy Thorson's Twitter thread of 13 Mar 2020: coyote time; jump buffering; halved gravity at the jump peak; jump and dash corner correction; lift momentum storage; wall jump from "2 pixels away from a wall"; super wall jump from "5 pixels away". Thorson: "All are centered around widening timing/positioning windows, so that everything is fudged a tiny bit in the player's favor."
- Discrepancy, re-checked: Player.cs's WallJumpCheck probes a Solid at an offset of WallJumpCheckDist = 3 px; if a hit needs the boxes to overlap (Monocle's usual rule, not opened here), that accepts walls up to 2 px away, which matches the thread's "2 pixels" (likely consistent; inference). Thorson hedged the super wall jump ("I think it's 5 pixels"), and in this Player.cs the super wall jump calls the same WallJumpCheck, so 5 px is UNVERIFIED in the source. The repo README says these class files are released "as a learning resource", not that they are the final shipped build. Thorson also notes Celeste runs at 320x180, so 2 px "is not small".
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: every hazard edge and timing window gets a small, invisible bias toward the player: hazard hitboxes a few px smaller than the art (steam, vent), drop windows ~0.1 s wider than the visual, the sentry's sight cone a little shorter than its drawn beam (never longer). This makes deaths feel fair without the player seeing any assist. NOTE (fact-check): drop_arm.gd currently does the opposite for the tank: a hit needs half the sentry's body over the brackets and "a graze at the edge of the brackets is a miss". Under this principle a graze in the player's favour should count as a hit.

### F19. Good deaths vs bad deaths, and the objective must be obvious (Halo 3 user research) [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-28]
- NPR (Wendy Kaufman, 24 Sep 2007), Microsoft Game Studios' Halo 3 testing: "there are good deaths and bad ones"; "Shooting yourself with a tank weapon or falling off a cliff you don't see can frustrate gamers." Randy Pagulayan: "people didn't know what they were supposed to be doing", fixed by flashing each new objective on screen; a weapon angle was changed because players shot themselves; "A barrier was installed on a highway after too many players were flying to their deaths." Goal: find "the discrepancy ... between what the player's actual experience is versus the designer's vision".
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: log every death with cause and x-position in test builds and look for clusters (NOTE, fact-check: the NPR transcript describes fixes made because "too many players" died the same way; it does not mention heatmaps or death logs, so "heatmap habit" is our inference; see also Celeste's DeathsInCurrentLevel counter). A cluster means a bad death (unseen hazard, unclear goal) and gets a level fix, not a difficulty fix.

### F20. Introduce danger safely first (Hayashida, Super Mario 3D Land) [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-32]
- Christian Nutt, Game Developer (13 Apr 2012): Hayashida's four-step kishōtenketsu level structure: "You introduce a concept, and then in the next panel you develop the idea a little bit more; in the third panel there's something of a change-up, and then in the fourth panel you have your conclusion." Each level has "a clear concept in the beginning". Hayashida: "First, you have to learn how to use that gameplay mechanic, and then the stage will offer you a slightly more complicated scenario in which you have to use it." (Secondary summaries say the first step is in a place where falling does not kill; that detail is UNVERIFIED in the opened article.)
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: each hazard is first met where it can only knock down, not kill: steam vent in a safe spot before the vent that guards a path; sentry seen patrolling from cover before it can chase; tank drop shown once before the player must time it. This is "warning before danger" as level structure.

### F21. Photosensitivity thresholds for death flashes and impact effects (W3C WCAG 2.3.1) [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-27]
- W3C, Understanding WCAG 2.2, SC 2.3.1 (Level A): nothing "flashes more than three times in any one second period", or flashes stay below the general and red flash thresholds (flash area within 25% of any 10-degree field, about 341 x 256 px at 1024 x 768). A general flash is a luminance change of 10% or more; red flashes count separately.
- ADDED in fact-check 2 [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-36], the game-specific platform rule, Xbox Accessibility Guideline 118 (Photosensitivity): a flash is "a 10% change in luminance"; failure when flashes occur "approximately more than three per second" and take up "approximately 20 percent or more" of the screen; a red flash counts when either extreme is a saturated red (R/(R+G+B) >= 0.8); "Lower intensity flashing can also cause a failure if it's continued for an extended period of time"; and removing such content "is preferred over splash-screen warnings". It names the Harding FPA test as a tool. Use XAG 118's ~20% screen-area figure for full-screen game effects (stricter in practice than WCAG's 341 x 256 px at 1024 x 768 block, which is a web-page rule).
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: the approved Cold Still death screen (no flashes) already complies. The impact kit's white flash plus red wash (impact.gd) and the sentry alert must stay at or below 3 flashes per second at all settings, with saturated red flashes avoided on full screen, and Reduced Motion must also shorten or remove them (it already shortens flashes).

### F22. The Last of Us Part II: per-category difficulty and failure bypasses (Naughty Dog, 2020) [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-29] [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-30]
- PlayStation.com (official): presets Very Light, Light, Moderate, Hard, Survivor plus Custom; five separate sliders: Player (damage taken, AND "Frequency of mid-encounter dynamic checkpoints"), Enemies (accuracy, projectile frequency), Allies (aggressiveness), Stealth ("Enemy's perception through vision, hearing, and smell" and "Length of grace period before enemies will alert others"), Resources. ADDED (fact-check): checkpoint density is itself a shipped difficulty lever in TLOU2, which supports the restore-point need; the Stealth grace period maps directly to the sentry's LOCK_SECONDS. Ledge Guard also "Prevents you from falling from ledges that will kill you." Failure bypasses: "Skip Puzzle" ("select SKIP PUZZLE from the pause menu to advance to the puzzle's completion"), Ledge Guard ("additional audio and vibration feedback"), Slow Motion, Lock-On Aim, hold/toggle and repeated-press-to-hold conversions, Traversal Assistance, Combat Audio Cues, High Contrast Display, "Invisible While Prone".
- Can I Play That? (Grant Stoner, 29 Jun 2020): "roughly 60 accessibility options"; Matthew Gallant: "It was absolutely critical that we planned for these features so early in development." The same interview (primary, not secondary) gives the timeline: "By the end of 2017, we had developed our first prototypes", and the first accessibility-centred tests ran "in the summer of 2018". Emilia Schatz on High Contrast: every interactable "had to be tagged with the right color".
- Fact-check 2 addition [FAILURE-DIFFICULTY-35]: AbleGamers' APX "Bypass" pattern ("The player can bypass part of the game so that they can continue to progress") cites Marvel's Spider-Man, where turning off QTEs makes "the former QTE sequences instead play out as cut scenes". That is the exact shape for a Junction skip: the blowback plays as a short scripted sequence, and the story and the trust beat are kept. Its "Save Early, Save Often" pattern backs the restore-point need.
- For WOLF//OVERRIDE: the direct models are a "Skip action beat" option (the Junction blowback is an action set piece, not the core story mechanic, so the guideline to allow bypassing non-core elements applies), a Stealth slider for the sentry's perception (SIGHT_RANGE, LOCK_SECONDS) and Ledge Guard for the walkable height change. Plan these now, as Naughty Dog did: hazards and interactables need tags from the start so High Contrast and the cues can be added later without repainting.

## Synthesis: what failure, retry and difficulty should be in WOLF//OVERRIDE

1. Death is information, not punishment, on the essential path. Juul (F2) and Miyazaki (F15) both say some failure raises enjoyment, but only when the player owns it (Juul; Meier F10). Every death needs a readable cause seen first (F19, F20), with small invisible margins in the player's favour (F9, F18).
2. Retry speed: keep the approved Cold Still timing (RETRY at 0.9 s, control at about 1.5 s; USE skips to control). Super Meat Boy's "almost instantaneous" respawn (F14) supports keeping it short; there is still no primary measured figure for Celeste or INSIDE. Respawn facing the goal ("goal always in sight", F14), with no audio gap (study).
3. Restore spacing: at most one beat and about 20 s of replay (study). After repeated deaths at the same beat, the restore point moves closer (Crash's crate, F4). Sources give NO death-count threshold; 3 deaths is a design value to test, UNVERIFIED.
4. After a death or a peak, guarantee a quiet window before the next threat. L4D relaxes for 30-45 s (F5). In a hand-built room, the respawn point is threat-free for at least a few seconds (design value).
5. Difficulty is a set of discrete assists the player owns, changeable any time with no progress loss (XAG 108, GAG, F7, F8): game speed 50-100% in 10% steps (Celeste F9), wider timing windows, hold-to-toggle, sentry calmer (Alien Novice levers, F16), and a Story assist where hazards only knock down. Names never judge the player (Celeste renamed "Cheat Mode", F9; XAG 108's "Wimp Mode" example).
6. Embedded active DDA is already the design: WOLF's help vs the engineer-only route (Chen F1). Do not add a prompt after each death asking to lower difficulty (Chen's Traffic Light result). An opt-in assist that grows per death (Hades, F6) is acceptable.
7. Time pressure is the most dangerous lever (Lomas F13): the tank-drop timing and any countdowns get the widest default windows and a non-timing alternative (GAG Advanced, F8). With HIT_RANGE 24 the derived windows are now about 0.34 s (patrol) and 0.21 s (chase), roughly half of what the study tuned against, and a miss costs a 6 s rewind. This is the most urgent tuning item in the slice (fact-check). Fact-check 2: the earlier study's own target table (design-reference-25-games.md, "Tank-drop window: >= today's ~0.63 s in the patrol case") is now failed by the code: 0.34 s is about half of it. For scale, Lomas tested time limits of 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 10, 15 and 30 s, and the shortest were the most disengaging.
8. Holds: default safe-point hold <=1.5 s, toggle option for all holds, crank progress survives release (already in hold_use.gd). XAG flags 2-3 s holds (F8).
9. Expect early attrition (F11): the first slice is where most players quit in every game measured. Make the first slice the fairest part of the game.
10. Mobile: >=44 pt targets (aim for 15 mm), press states. On an interruption, auto-pause in place (already in main.gd) and let them "continue as if they never left" (Apple HIG Multitasking, F17); the last autosave is only the fallback if the app is killed (F17, F8).
11. Measure: log deaths by cause and position in test builds (F19, F3).

## Agreement / disagreement with design-reference-25-games.md
- AGREE: section 4 principle 1 ("Death must be readable before it happens") is strengthened by Juul's attribution result (F2), Meier (F10), Halo's good/bad deaths (F19) and Celeste's forgiveness margins (F9/F18).
- AGREE: death-to-control <= ~1.45-1.5 s with a skip. Super Meat Boy (F14) supports it; the Celeste "~2.0-2.1 s / 1.1 s skipped" figure remains derived, and I found no primary measured number either (still UNVERIFIED).
- AGREE: replay <=20 s / <=1 beat. Add the Crash rule: the restore point moves closer after repeated deaths (F4).
- PARTLY DISAGREE: "Secure action: Hold USE ~1-3 s" (section 4 targets, section 12). XAG 107 flags holds of 2-3 s or more and asks for toggles (F8). For the required first-corridor safe point, keep the default <=1.5 s and add a hold/toggle option; Alien's 3 s is a horror-tension precedent, not an accessibility-safe default.
- DISAGREE (gap): the study has no easy/assist mode at all and lists only "Optional hard mode with scarce saves" for later. XAG 108, the Game Accessibility Guidelines, Celeste, TLOU2 (around 60 options, 5 per-category difficulty sliders, Skip Puzzle), Hades and Alien's post-launch Novice patch (F7, F9, F16, TLOU2 notes) all argue that assists belong in the first playable slice, especially because mobile is a first-slice target and the study itself warns about "launching with one accessibility toggle" (Planet of Lana). Today the slice has Reduced Motion only.
- EXTEND: the study's "Optional low-death recognition (Limbo 'five or fewer deaths')". The measured rate is 2.5% (F11), so treat it as a rare-badge reward only, never a target.
- EXTEND: the study has no DDA section. My position: no hidden difficulty change on the first death; in-fiction, small, iterative help only after repeated deaths at one beat (Hamlet F3, Crash F4); otherwise change pacing, not difficulty (L4D F5); the player-chosen route is the main DDA (Chen F1).
- AGREE: section 10 item 10 (sentry chase 230 > 190 is the unreadable death). Code now has LOCK_SECONDS = 0.5 (an alert beat), which is a partial fix; Alien's Novice levers (F16) are the right knobs for an assist.
- AGREE: the Cold Still death screen has no flashes; WCAG 2.3.1 (F21) gives the hard limit for the remaining impact flashes.
- NOTE: the study's sentry/tank numbers are out of date. drop_arm.gd HIT_RANGE is now 24 (the study says 44), so the derived 0.63 s / 0.38 s tank windows need recomputing.

## Pitfalls
- Deaths the player can't attribute to their own play (unseen hazard, random timing, beam shorter than the real sight range); players blame the game (Meier) and enjoy it less (Juul).
- Tight time windows as the main challenge (Lomas: most disengaging factor); the tank drop in chase mode.
- Holds of 2-3 s or more without a toggle (XAG 107); button mashing for the valve.
- A "make it easier?" popup after each death (Chen's Traffic Light broke flow); assist names that shame ("Cheat", "Wimp").
- Hidden DDA that changes the room on the first death or is noticeable (Hamlet: interventions can "interrupt their overall suspension of disbelief").
- Losing essential progress to death or to an app interruption on mobile.
- Respawning facing away from the goal, or right into the threat with no quiet window.
- Full-screen red/white flashes faster than 3 per second.
- Treating zero deaths as the goal: only 2.5% of Limbo owners finished with five or fewer deaths.
- Shipping the lowest difficulty tier as a patch (Alien: Isolation launched with Easy/Normal/Hard and still added an even lower Novice in a Dec 2014 update).
