## Findings

### F1. Valve, Half-Life (1998): "experiential density", distance-based content [LOOP-PACING-1]
- Birdwell (Game Developer, Dec 1999): theory of "experiential density — the amount of 'things' that happen to and are done by the player per unit of time and area of a map".
- "all content is distance based, not time based, and no activities are started outside the player's control. If the players are in the mood for more action, all they need to do is move forward and within a few seconds something will happen."
- "If the world ignores the player, the player won't care about the world."
- Numbers: Cabal met 4 days/week, 6 h/day for 5 months; >200 playtest sessions; a 2-hour playtest produced ~100 "action items".
- WOLF: trigger beats by the engineer's position (x), never by a clock. Standing still should be safe (except inside a deliberately timed set piece). Every USE, crank and choice gets a visible world response.

### F2. Left 4 Dead AI Director: measured tension/release cycle [LOOP-PACING-2]
- Booth, "The AI Systems of Left 4 Dead" (Valve slides; Valve's publications page lists the venue as the Artificial Intelligence and Interactive Digital Entertainment Conference, Stanford, 2009; 'keynote' not confirmed). Observation from Counter-Strike: "Constant, unchanging combat is fatiguing"; "Long periods of inactivity are boring".
- Phases: Build Up (full threat until intensity crosses peak) -> Sustain Peak "for 3-5 seconds after Survivor Intensity has peaked" -> Peak Fade (minimal threat until intensity decays) -> Relax "minimal threat population for 30-45 seconds, or until Survivors have traveled far enough toward the next safe room".
- Mob spawns "at randomized interval between 90 and 180 seconds"; a separate slide says "90-180 seconds on Normal difficulty". Bosses are NOT under adaptive pacing ("intended to change up the pacing anyhow").
- WOLF: the best published numeric tension-release cycle. A Blowback peak should be short (seconds), followed by a ~30-45 s low-threat stretch before the next build-up; the release can also be distance-gated (reach the next safe point). Authored set pieces (the tank drop) are the "boss" exception.

### F3. Valve, Half-Life 2 developer commentary (20th anniversary update, 2024) [LOOP-PACING-3]
- David Speyrer (cn_104, washing-machine puzzle): added "late in development, after playtesting indicated that players needed some downtime from the relentless pressure from the helicopter. Pacing contrasts like this can accentuate the high moments by resetting the player's emotional state and warding off exhaustion or even boredom." Without it "fatigue set in which quickly started to feel like monotony rather than excitement."
- Dario Casali (cn_212): Eli's Lab to Ravenholm was "far too abrupt"; "they were immediately dropped into combat with zombies without any room for tension building", so they added a section and "slowed the pacing, focused on art and lighting".
- Danika Rogers (cn_189, Nova Prospekt): after "two combat-heavy levels, so we wanted to slow things down once they got inside", and introduced turrets "first as an enemy to the player and later as a tool".
- Steve Bond (cn_229): after training, shift "from moody claustrophobic spaces to more combat focused action arenas"; (cn_235) an art-led plaza where "playtesters tended to fly right through it" needed threats added.
- Yahn Bernier (cn_228): junk-filled hallways "a simple solution to our need to slow the player down", justified in-fiction by Grigori.
- Matt Boone (cn_054, bridge): tension "visible in their movements"; on the return trip "Concerned they might be bored on the second trip, we decided to add a gunship". -> forced backtracking gets new content.
- Dhabih Eng (cn_152): in choreo scenes "we felt like we needed a constant stream of interesting details on screen", "essentially toys for the player to tinker with" — but "too much visual interest could distract players".
- Dave Riller (cn_211, Autosave Dangerous): never autosave where the player can die right after loading ("death loop"); save is committed only if the player survives a set time, "like thirty seconds".
- Tom Leonard (cn_237): weekly over-the-shoulder playtests; late-stage tests with beginners.
- Jeff Lane (cn_105_airboat_changeup): playtesters "wanted more varied gameplay from the airboat", so "it felt right to introduce a new weapon, and in turn deliver a pacing change and set the stage for what would come next", followed by target practice.
- Dave Riller (cn_094_airboat_time): driving fast meant players "were missing out on exploring", so they "added periodic spots where players could get out and explore small pockets of dense detail".
- Adrian Finol (cn_113_blocking_tutorials): gates where the player must show they learned something are disguised "as something natural, so it doesn't feel like the game has come to a halt"; a simple stacking puzzle taught better than complex designs.
- Charlie Brown (cn_115_trainstation): the opening must make players "quickly grasp what's happening, who the key players are, what threats they'll face"; "above all else, it has to be fun and as interactive as possible."
- Erik Johnson (cn_193_final_turret_arena): build the arc's endpoint first, playtest it, "Then, we work backward, adding preceding scenarios to introduce the element and build player skill."
- Mike Dussault (cn_083_barnacle_introduction; FACT-CHECK: speaker corrected from Josh Weier): new threats shown first "at a safe distance" in a "'nature show' style predator/prey scene".
- Randy Lundeen (Citadel): after the climactic battle, "a different tone for the finale", "alternating between spectacle and unleashed power".
- WOLF: every high (sentry, overload) needs a designed low right after it (a quiet puzzle, records reading, a WOLF moment). Insert a slow, art-and-sound "tension building" stretch before a new threat type; never drop the player straight into it. If the route sends the engineer back over ground, add something new on the return. Safe points must not commit inside danger (Autosave Dangerous logic).

### F4. Uncharted 2 (Naughty Dog): difficulty spikes break pacing; set pieces are expensive [LOOP-PACING-4]
- Lemarchand, GDC 2010 "Among Friends: An Uncharted 2 Post-mortem" (reported by Ludwig Kietzmann, Joystiq/Engadget, 2010-03-12: "just ask the playtester who repeated the train yard shootout 27 times"): one playtester repeated the train-yard shootout 27 times -> retuned to "a less intimidating challenge". Set pieces predicted in pre-production as "enormously time-consuming"; the train level "an evil monster of a level". The monastery: "by the time a sluggish pace had made itself known, too much final art had been committed" (reporter's wording; FACT-CHECK: was misquoted as "sluggish pacing").
- Lemarchand also gave GDC 2012 "Attention, Not Immersion" (GDC Vault 1015464): playtesting and metrics to understand player attention. (Abstract opened; talk video not watched — content beyond the abstract UNVERIFIED.)
- Druckmann, "Postmortem: Uncharted: Drake's Fortune" (Game Developer, 2008-10-08): "tightly focused on a few core mechanics"; "If we hadn't been so pressed for time, we would have iterated each level many more times before we moved on to the stage of creating finished environment art."
- Benson Russell, "Designing Combat Encounters in Uncharted 2" (Game Developer, 2010-08-03): "achieving a balance amongst the different gameplay mechanics, picking ones that best support the story while evolving things for the player"; climbing routes usable by both player and enemy; quick informal tests by grabbing nearby colleagues ("fellow designers, mainly") under focus-testing rules. (FACT-CHECK: the earlier claim 'playtest with non-core players' is not in the article and was dropped.)
- WOLF: grey-box and play pacing before painting (matches the decided grey-box-then-paint rule). Track retries per beat: a beat that needs many retries (27 is a red flag; target: most fresh players clear a beat in <=3 tries — derived, not sourced) is a pacing failure, not a challenge.

### F5. Another World (Eric Chahi, 1991): rhythm "like a music score", short cinematic punctuation [LOOP-PACING-5]
- Christian Nutt, "GDC 2011: Eric Chahi's Retro Postmortem: Another World" (Game Developer, 2011-03-03). Chahi placed enemy laser beams "to give different rhythm to modulate the tension, a little bit like a music score" (Chahi's words). Nutt's report (his paraphrase, not a Chahi quote): cinematic punctuation, "brief sequences only", that in Chahi's quoted words "give emphasis to a major event"; the first beast encounter gives the first level "a slow rhythm before creature arrives, and a quick change to a fast and stressful situation" (reporter's wording).
- 17 months to finish one third of the game (Dec 1990); release 1991.
- WOLF: cutscenes are punctuation (a few seconds), not chapters. Open each room slow, then flip fast (the slug-then-beast pattern) — the junction should start quiet so the first sentry sighting lands.

### F6. Kishōtenketsu (Nintendo, Koichi Hayashida): one idea per stage, four steps [LOOP-PACING-6]
- Christian Nutt, "The Secret to Mario Level Design" (Game Developer, 2012-04-13), Hayashida: "First, you have to learn how to use that gameplay mechanic, and then the stage will offer you a slightly more complicated scenario in which you have to use it. And then the next step is something crazy happens that makes you think about it in a way you weren't expecting. And then you get to demonstrate, finally, what sort of mastery you've gained over it."
- WOLF: each room should own one idea and run it intro -> develop -> twist -> payoff. The Service Junction already does: overload a line (intro) -> crank the valve (develop) -> blast the door (twist) -> drop the tank / dump into the exit bolt (mastery). The corridor: breaker (intro) -> relay with/without WOLF (develop) -> manual bypass under alarm (twist) -> sealed door opens (payoff).

### F7. Brothers (Josef Fares/Starbreeze, 2013): keep the pace going, never repeat [LOOP-PACING-7]
- Game Informer "Afterwords: Brothers: A Tale of Two Sons" (2013-09-27), Fares: "It was very important to me to keep the pace going. Every puzzle and challenge needed to be quite soft and not too hard"; "We just wanted to go the opposite all the time"; on one-off animation content: "The amount of content animation required just to be used there was really crazy from a game perspective."
- Kyle Hilliard, Game Informer "Games To Beat In A Day" (2014-09-01): about 3 hours; best in one sitting (journalist opinion).
- WOLF: approachable puzzles keep the pace; spend animation budget on one-off beats rather than reusing a mechanic until it is stale.

### F8. Little Nightmares (Tarsier, 2017): quality over length [LOOP-PACING-8]
- Game Informer "Little Nightmares Afterwords" (2017-05-30), Dave Mervik: "We never really wanted to make a long game. The idea always was to make something that got in and got out ... the emphasis was on quality rather than length." Future length "would never be just for the sake of it."
- WOLF: a 3-5 h campaign target is in line with the genre; never pad (no fetch loops, no repeated crossings) to reach it.

### F9. Jeppe Carlsen (Limbo/INSIDE lead gameplay designer; Cocoon): never stay in one gear [LOOP-PACING-9]
- Premortem.games interview by Eric Bartelson (2024-09-18), about Cocoon (not Limbo/INSIDE): Carlsen's quoted pacing challenge was "to minimize frustration and to make sure that the player does not get exhausted from having too many worlds available"; his solution was to mix linear sections, open world-jumping sections and tense boss fights, so that, in Bartelson's own words (FACT-CHECK 2: a paraphrase, not a Carlsen quote), "the game never stays in one specific gear for too long." A puzzle should also "preps the mindset of the player for a more interesting puzzle later in the game." "The one thing I never compromise on is playability. The art, sounds, and animation need to communicate the puzzle logic effectively."
- WOLF: rotate modes (walk/listen, repair puzzle, WOLF conversation, action) so no mode runs long; early simple uses of a device prepare a harder use later (the corridor's breaker/relay prepares the junction overload).

### F11. Valve, Half-Life 2: Episode One (2006) and Episode Two (2007) developer commentary [LOOP-PACING-11]
(Transcripts on Combine OverWiki, read from the wiki source; speakers as listed there.)
- Fatigue -> change of mode, found by playtest, again and again:
  - Gautam Babbar (Ep1 hospitalrespite): "Too much of the same type of uninterrupted gameplay causes fatigue." Players "were ready for a break from intense combat", so "this relatively calm exploration puzzle" was inserted.
  - John Guthrie (Ep1 airventcollapse): "After the antlion guard fight, playtesters were reporting some combat fatigue. We decided to include an environmental puzzle here to break up the pacing".
  - Charlie Burgin (Ep1 spacerefinement): a side passage "provided a short break in the combat tension after the boss battle".
  - Eric Strand (Ep2 055): players "were fairly tired of antlion combat at this point", so "a complete break from antlions".
  - Matt Rhoten (Ep2 156): a second driving section was cut because "The pacing felt wrong".
  - Charlie Burgin (Ep2 045): "We like to lull players into a trance, then startle them." (explore/puzzle -> abrupt zombine attack)
- Companion must not set the pace:
  - Matt T. Wood (Ep1 alyxnag): Alyx's "Hurry up!" nags — "after about three minutes of this, the one thing it definitely did was make the player hate Alyx"; "players much preferred to set the pace themselves", so Alyx switched "from generally leading players to almost always following".
  - Bill Van Buren (Ep1 alyxcitadeltone): Alyx "is always content to proceed at whatever pace the player chooses"; impatient lines were cut.
- Backtracking:
  - Ido Magal (Ep1 sealingoffcafe): "when we limit the amount of backtracking players can do, it gives them confidence to venture forward."
  - Ido Magal (Ep1 escortmap): deliberate "temporary backtracking" through the same area so players had "full tactical awareness" — repeated ground works only when the second pass has a new purpose.
- Openings, goals and chapter seams:
  - Gautam Babbar (Ep2 008): opening in the crashed train lets players "reacquaint themselves with the basic movement controls in a safe, contained environment". Earlier design had precarious jumps: "most playtesters fall to their deaths numerous times in the first five minutes", so "we optimized for perceived rather than actual danger."
  - Erik Johnson (Ep2 002): a transmission scene "added fairly late" because playtesters "weren't sure where they were going, or why"; set the goal "right away".
  - Lars Jensvold (Ep2 105): a short scene restating goals placed after puzzles and combat "feels like a small reward".
  - Lars Jensvold (Ep1 finalereqs): at the episode's ending "Alyx had to be mostly silent during this scene or else players tended to look at her instead of all of the chaos around them."
  - Charlie Brown (Ep1 firstvista): "spots where players can take a break to look out over an expanse of cool scenery. We call these 'vistas'."
- Set-piece tuning for a near miss:
  - Mike Durand (Ep2 075): to make the player "just barely make it through" before the antlion guardian arrives, "we had to reduce the number of boards again and again until we saw playtesters consistently scrambling to safety at the last moment."
  - Jason Ruyman (Ep2 077): players dropped past the beat and "Nobody ever saw the guardian break through the boards", so the layout forced a turn to see it.
  - Vitaly Genkin (Ep2 067): a gear-plus-crank puzzle had "most playtesters got stuck here for many minutes. We kept simplifying".
  - Gautam Babbar (Ep1 followthewire): a fight "that lasted several minutes" while waiting for an elevator; level made smaller "to ensure that the player would see the elevator and recognize the problem that needed to be solved before the fight started."
  - Jeff Sorenson (Ep2 112): an area "appears open and non-linear" but is laid out for "a fairly reliable progression of events, so that we can fine tune pacing".
  - David Speyrer (Ep2 177): clues before an ambush "create a sense of growing apprehension".
- WOLF: (1) WOLF never hurries the engineer and never nags (three minutes of nagging destroyed Alyx's likeability); his urgency shows through his own body and the world, not lines pushing the player. (2) Seal behind after a threshold (the corridor seal, the blown junction door) and say so visually; a forced return must have a new purpose. (3) Every room shows its goal before its threat starts (see the exit bolt before the sentry hunts). (4) Tune the Blowback timing beats until fresh players scramble through "at the last moment" rather than until they fail; perceived danger over real danger in the first five minutes. (5) Chapter ends: a peak, then a quiet image where WOLF is silent and the world speaks; the next chapter opens in a safe, contained space that restates the goal.

### F12. Halo (Bungie, Jaime Griesemer): nested 3 s / 30 s / 3 min loops [LOOP-PACING-12]
- Ludwig Kietzmann, "Half-Minute Halo: An Interview with Jaime Griesemer" (Joystiq/Engadget, 2011-07-14). Original line: "In Halo 1, there was maybe 30 seconds of fun that happened over and over and over and over again." His clarification: "you have a 3-second loop inside of a 30-second loop inside of a 3-minute loop that is always different"; "No 30 second stretch of Halo is ever repeated; the missions are constantly changing the context on you".
- WOLF translation (derived): 3 s = one action and its visible response (step, USE, crank tick, WOLF reaction); 30 s = one beat (read a terminal, ask WOLF, crank the valve under alarm, dodge one sentry pass); 3 min = one room's idea (corridor, Records, junction) that must never repeat the previous room's context.

### F13. Naughty Dog: quiet moments as counterpoint; mix ratios [LOOP-PACING-13]
- Amy Hennig, Game Informer "Storytellers of the Decade" interview by Meagan Marie (2010-12-02): "working on the pacing and making sure that there aren't dead points or frustration points, as much as we can, but a lot of it has to do with wanting to see what happens next because you care about the characters."
- Evan Wells to Kotaku (Brian Crecente, 2009-08-21): Uncharted 2 aimed at "60% action, 40% adventure" across "gun combat, hand to hand melee, puzzle solving, traversing, exploring, and vehicle sections".
- Simon Parkin (critic), "Analysis: One Year On - Naughty Dog's Uncharted 2" (Game Developer, 2010-09-29): the mid-game village is "a composer-like approach to pacing, the developer setting bars of rest after bars of fury"; after maximal action "the only way to maintain it is with a period of inaction". (Critic, not developer.)
- Neil Druckmann to The Telegraph (2018-06, paywalled; quoted via Wccftech): "without it it would feel relentless ... you need those moments of reprieve, you need to see those moments of happiness, of dancing, of humour". Original not opened: UNVERIFIED wording.
- Marianne Hayden, GDC 2019 "Design vs. Story: How 'Uncharted: The Lost Legacy' Addressed the Elephant in the Room" (abstract): playtesters all wanted to ride the elephant; it became a "full, peaceful story moment".
- WOLF: WOLF//OVERRIDE's dark tone needs authored reprieve (WOLF play, small humour, the sunrise petting payoff later). Proposed slice mix (derived, to be measured): roughly 40% tension/action, 60% exploration/puzzle/story — the inverse of Uncharted, because this is a cinematic narrative game, not an action-adventure; confirm with a stopwatch playtest.

### F14. A Plague Tale: Requiem (Asobo): opening up costs narrative rhythm [LOOP-PACING-14]

### F15. Mobile sessions and platform guidance [LOOP-PACING-15]
- GameAnalytics 2025 Mobile Gaming Benchmarks (11,600 games, iOS+Android, 2024 data), as summarised by Dmitriy Byshonkov (Game Dev Reports, 2025-02-17): "the average session length for the top 25% of projects in the market was 8-9 minutes. The median is 5-6 minutes"; "On average, a player has 4 sessions per day"; median daily playtime 22 min. (FACT-CHECK 2: the primary report PDF was OCR'd page by page and now confirms these numbers: "session lengths among the top 25% of games averaged between 8 and 9 minutes, while the median 50% of games reported session lengths between 5 and 6 minutes"; "the median session count across all mobile games was 4 sessions per day". The report itself says this reflects a market where "casual and hyper-casual games dominate", and that "Midcore games ... tend to range between 10-15 minutes". Report landing page: https://www.gameanalytics.com/reports/2025-mobile-gaming-benchmarks; the PDF opened was the identical file mirrored at investgame.net.)
- GameAnalytics, "2026 Mobile & PC Gaming Benchmarks" (2025 data; added by fact-check 2) [LOOP-PACING-15d]: mobile sessions got SHORTER. "Median games (P50) consistently recorded session lengths between 3.1 and 3.5 minutes"; "The top 25% of games (P75) averaged around 5.2 minutes per session, while the top 10% (P90) reached just over 8 minutes"; median 3.8-3.9 sessions per day. On PC, "median session length (P50) remained highly stable at around 18 minutes per session, while the top 25% of games (P75) consistently exceeded 30 minutes." The same report quotes a founder (Oxana Fomina, Gradient Universe, not GameAnalytics data): "If the value is not felt in the first 10–15 minutes, the player is gone forever."
- Apple Human Interface Guidelines, "Launching" (accessed 2026-10-12; FACT-CHECK: the 'retrace steps' line is about restoring state on relaunch, so it supports exact resume, not level-design rules about backtracking): "Launch instantly. People want to start interacting with your app or game right away, and sometimes they don't want to wait more than a couple of seconds." "Restore the previous state when your app restarts so people can continue where they left off. Avoid making people retrace steps to reach their previous location in your app or game."
- Apple HIG, "Designing for games": "Teach through play ... a playable tutorial that engages people quickly and helps them feel successful right away."
- WOLF: on a phone a sitting is minutes, not hours. A natural stopping point (a safe point or room exit) should come at least every ~5 minutes of play (FACT-CHECK 2: tightened from 5-10 min; derived from the 2025-data mobile median of 3.1-3.5 min and P75 of ~5.2 min [15d]; these are mostly casual games, so treat as a floor, not a target, and a PC sitting is ~18 min median), and resume must put the engineer back where they were (room + beat), never at the chapter start. First input within seconds of New Game; the opening's skippable cinematic must stay short.

### F16. Limbo (Jeppe Carlsen, GDC Europe 2010): trial and error must stay cheap [LOOP-PACING-16]
- Brandon Sheffield, Game Developer report of Carlsen's GDC Europe talk (2010-08-16): "learning-by-dying"; "You don't ever really get penalized for dying, it's just how the game plays"; make wrong approaches "clearly present that they are wrong" because "it takes the player a long time to go back to an idea that he had previously discarded"; "It's very important that the correct solution is fairly easy to execute."

### F17. Valve, Portal (2007) developer commentary: one new concept at a time, simple timed tasks between hard thinking [LOOP-PACING-17]
- Garret Rickey (prtl-004): "fatigue set in if we didn't break up the more complicated, deliberately paced puzzles with problems that required the player to perform a much simpler task under time pressure."
- Chris Chin (prtl-040): a puzzle "introduced too many new concepts at once, which ended up frustrating a lot of playtesters", so "we inserted two test chambers before this one".
- Kim Swift (prtl-069): "Overwhelmed players tend not to digest new information, so we simplified the puzzle to require only the double fling."
- Robin Walker (prtl-016/023): Portal "is effectively an extended player training exercise", layering tools into harder puzzles; early versions "let players stumble through the beginning ... without always understanding", which "compromised teaching new concepts".
- Jason Brashill (prtl-024): "a mandatory pause in the action - what we call a gate" so players notice the new thing; (prtl-058) "Adding a ticking timer sound when the door was open cued players that the puzzle expected them to act during that time".
- Chris Chin (prtl-057): "Player training doesn't always stick, especially after the introduction of a big, new concept", so a skill is reintroduced.
- Realm Lovejoy (prtl-096): an old chamber solved "in a new way" after the escape makes revisiting feel like progress.
- WOLF: the Service Junction stacks five verbs (overload, crank, blast, drop, dump). Each must have been met once, alone and safely, before it is combined (the corridor's MANUAL BYPASS hold-to-crank teaches the crank; the coolant relay teaches the coolant line). Max one new concept per beat; a beat that combines two new things needs a teaching beat in front. Use a ticking/alarm sound to say "act now" in the timed parts.

## Synthesis: what the player does every 10 s, every minute, every 10 minutes (WOLF//OVERRIDE)

All "derived" values below are design inferences from the sourced numbers, to be checked with a stopwatch in hands-on play.

| Scale | What great games do | Numbers and sources | WOLF//OVERRIDE target (derived) |
|---|---|---|---|
| ~3-10 s | One action with a visible, audible response; something happens "within a few seconds" of moving forward | Birdwell "within a few seconds" [1]; Griesemer 3 s loop [12]; HL2 "if the world ignores the player" [1] | Every USE, crank tick, choice and step-into-trigger answers within the same beat (sound + motion). While moving, no more than ~5-10 s of jog (~950-1,900 px at 190 px/s) without new information: a sound, a light change, a WOLF glance, a reveal. At walk speed 80 px/s the same ~5-10 s is only 400-800 px, but walking lasts only ~0.5 s of a held input before the jog, so a held crossing of one view is ~4.0 s (0.5 s x 80 px/s = 40 px, then 671 px at 190 px/s = 3.5 s; fact-check 2 corrected from ~3.9 s). |

### F18 (added by fact-checker). Mark Davies: pace = movement impetus, threat, tension, tempo [LOOP-PACING-18]
- Davies, "Examining Game Pace: How Single-Player Levels Tick" (Game Developer, 2009-05-12). Splits pace into "Movement Impetus -- the will of the player to move through the level", "Threat", "Tension -- the atmosphere and mood of the level or perceived danger" and "Tempo -- the level of actual action". Case study, Call of Duty 4 "All Ghillied Up": in a 10-minute section "the tempo is never extremely high ... but the pace was maintained constantly by having either threat, tension or movement impetus high at every point."
- Things that slow a player: "Increased tension" (Dead Space players "move slowly to ready themselves"), "Multiple Routes / Open World -- choice requires thought and thought slows movement impetus."
- Same level: "a scripted AI buddy to literally drive the pace at every moment", which "isn't generally possible to do this in most level designs".
- WOLF: this is a practitioner analysis, not a postmortem, but it gives the clearest working definition of dead time: a stretch where impetus, threat AND tension are all low. It supports the researcher's "no new information AND no rising tension" rule. It also adds nuance to the Alyx finding: a companion leading can carry pace in a scripted follow sequence, where following is the player's goal. Valve's failure was nagging and urging, not leading as such. WOLF can lead on an authored route (a crawlway, a vent), but he never urges or waits impatiently.
