## Findings

### Super Mario Bros. World 1-1 and Nintendo's teaching rules
- [ONBOARDING-1] Iwata Asks, New Super Mario Bros. Wii, Vol. 1 part 4 "Letting Everyone Know It Was A Good Mushroom" (Iwata with Miyamoto, 2009). Miyamoto: "you encounter a Goomba right at the start"; Iwata: "If you play the game for the first time with no prior knowledge, you're going to run into the first Goomba and lose a turn." [quote corrected in fact-check] Miyamoto: "you have to teach the player in a natural way that they need to avoid them by jumping over them"; players who mistime the jump stamp on it and so learn stomping. The mushroom moves right, hits the pipe and comes back; with the block overhead "whatever you do, you'll get the mushroom" (Iwata), designed so players learn the item is good. Mushrooms escape "at a bit slower speed than you" so chasing is fun (Miyamoto).
  - Numbers: first lesson = 1 enemy, 1 verb (jump), death allowed (lose a turn); 1 forced item pickup.
  - WOLF//OVERRIDE: the first threat should be met alone, on flat ground, with one verb that solves it; the first good thing (a WOLF act of help, a record) should come to the engineer even if they try to avoid it. A mistake in the first lesson can cost, but must teach (cause visible).
- [ONBOARDING-2] Iwata Asks, NSMB Wii, Vol. 1 part 5 (2009): Super Guide exists so novices can see the end; Miyamoto would be "intensely irritated" to be offered it "right from the start of the game". Help is offered after failure, never before play.
- [ONBOARDING-3] Iwata Asks, Super Mario Bros. 25th Anniversary, Vol. 3 "Super Mario Developers" part 3 (Nintendo developers Konno and Eguchi, 2010): Miyamoto taught "introducing game elements in order", one by one, never all at once; Konno: "First there's a basic instance, then a developed instance, and then an advanced, combined instance. He often said, 'Use one element three times.'"
  - Numbers: 3 uses per element; 1 new element at a time.
  - Fact-check note: in Nintendo's version the third use is already the "advanced, combined instance"; in Valve's version (Dalton, [ONBOARDING-11]) the third use "challenges them in a new way using that mechanism". They differ on whether combining is the third step or comes after it. Both agree on one new element at a time and about three uses.
- [ONBOARDING-4] Same volume, part 5 "Keep Moving Right and You'll Eventually Reach the Goal" (Kimura, Eguchi, 2010): "move to the right" "provides players with a sense of relief, so they can start the game without thinking too much about what has to be done."
  - WOLF//OVERRIDE: one direction of travel (right) for the first rooms; the goal side of each room is the right side; backtracking only when the room says so (the refusal fallback).
- [ONBOARDING-5] Christian Nutt, "The Secret to Mario Level Design", Game Developer (Gamasutra), 2012-04-13, interviewing Koichi Hayashida (director, Super Mario 3D Land / Galaxy 2): four steps from kishotenketsu: learn the mechanic; "a slightly more complicated scenario"; a twist "that makes you think about it in a way you weren't expecting"; then "demonstrate ... what sort of mastery you've gained". Hayashida credits Miyamoto, who "drew comics as a kid".

### Portal (Valve, 2007): gated, layered training, measured by weekly playtests
- [ONBOARDING-6] Valve, Portal developer commentary (in-game, 2007; transcript on the Portal Wiki). Robin Walker (Ch. 00): "Portal is effectively an extended player training exercise"; tools introduced, then layered; the button/box mechanic is trained before portals. Kim Swift (Ch. 00): players "quickly wrap their heads around" portals when they see themselves through one, so the first portal is placed so players "will invariably see themselves"; a visually unique first room (radio) gives a reference point. Robin Walker (Ch. 01): "Early versions of Portal let players stumble through the beginning ... without always understanding"; the puzzle is built so stumbling "will virtually always lead to a dead end" and needs "a minimum of five portals in a specific order" (gating). Jason Brashill (Ch. 02): playtesters charged past; Valve added "a mandatory pause in the action - what we call a gate", with particles and a loud noise to point attention. Chet Faliszek (Ch. 04): "there's generally just one correct solution to these early puzzles"; a clever bypass was treated as "a failure" and blocked with glass; later puzzles open up. Chris Chin (Ch. 08): one chamber "introduced too many new concepts at once"; Valve "inserted two test chambers before this one". Chris Chin (Ch. 12): "Player training doesn't always stick", so a skill is reintroduced after a big new concept. Garret Rickey (Ch. 10): momentum was "the hardest concept to convey"; only there did the AI voice explain explicitly, "something we avoided throughout most of the rest of the game". Greg Coomer (Ch. 10): a repeated visual cue (pushed-out slab over checkerboard) teaches flinging by association. Kim Swift (Ch. 15): "Overwhelmed players tend not to digest new information". Paul Graham (Ch. 01): "we never kill them" inside a closing portal (early safety). Jeep Barnett (Ch. 07): killing players on touching the rails was "too much of an over correction", because even skilled testers were frustrated by the one-hit kills "in the more complex puzzles later in the game" (so this is about later puzzles, not the first minutes). Jason Brashill (Ch. 11): a ticking timer sound told players to act in a window. Paul Graham (Ch. 19): a wall needed a visible side or players "panicked".
  - Numbers: 1 new concept per chamber; 2 chambers inserted when one taught too much; gate = 5 portals in order; 1 correct solution early.
  - WOLF//OVERRIDE: the corridor is the training room. One concept per beat (move; USE; hold-USE; choice), a gate where the player must stop and look (the seal opening), no clever bypasses in the first room, and explicit spoken instruction kept for the one concept that is genuinely hard (the tank-drop timing), never for walking or USE.
- [ONBOARDING-7] Brandon Sheffield, "Still Alive: Kim Swift And Erik Wolpaw Talk Portal", Game Developer (Gamasutra), 2008-03-25. Swift: "we playtested our game every single week"; Wolpaw: Portal "was being playtested in the first week"; a level goes in "in two to five days". Swift on "time-to-crate" (the interviewer's joke term): "I guess it would be like a minute and five seconds, in time-to-crate. We trap you in that first room for a minute! (laughs)"; Wolpaw: "There's an artificial trapping, so we could boost that time-to-crate up." Players were asked "to tell us the story back"; Wolpaw: if testers missed story it "served us better usually to cut it", because adding more made people tune out.
  - Numbers: first cube ~65 s after start (Swift's offhand estimate, not a measurement); 1 playtest per week; 2-5 days level-to-test.
  - Fact-check caveat: in Portal the player has movement control from the first second; the ~65 s is time to the first object, not time to first control. It does NOT support a "first control within 60 s" target; it supports "first interactive object within about a minute of control".
  - WOLF//OVERRIDE: test the story the same way (ask testers to tell the WOLF/Director setup back); time-to-first-verb is a tracked number.
- [ONBOARDING-8] Chris Remo, "Valve's Faliszek: Playtesters Aren't Idiots, It's You", Game Developer, 2008-08-07: Faliszek describes "stages of denial" across successive tester groups: by "the third or fourth time" (the third or fourth group seen failing the same way) "you're realizing, 'I'm an idiot ... It's not their fault, it's our fault.'" (It is about repeated groups, not one tester's third attempt.); "Playtesting is just proof to you"; a small texture change behind a ladder in Left 4 Dead broke whole playtests.
- [ONBOARDING-9] Mike Ambinder (Valve), "Valve's Approach to Playtesting: The Application of Empiricism", GDC 2009 slides (Valve CDN PDF): "Game designs are hypotheses", "Playtests are experiments"; "Importance of what people do—not what they say"; think-aloud; death heatmaps and level times as stats; "Nothing beats direct gameplay observation".

### Half-Life 2 and its Episodes (Valve): training as a "tenet", companion hints, safe first minutes
Source: Valve developer commentary (HL2 commentary recorded for the 20th Anniversary Update, 2024; Episode One 2006; Episode Two 2007), transcripts on Combine OverWiki.
- [ONBOARDING-10] HL2 commentary (2024). Matt Wright (cn_022 "hands free"): HL2 starts the player "empty-handed"; "Without a weapon, it was easier to train the player on the more novel ways they're able to interact with the world and introduce them to our story." Eric Kirchmer (cn_018): where "players lost track of their goal", small interstitial scenes with characters worked "much better than relying on passive storytelling or even some sort of reminder you'd find baked into a pause menu." David Sawyer (cn_036): "we aim to add enough flavor and context to training so it doesn't feel like training"; without training, testers used the old habit (shot the antlion). Eric Smith (cn_039): HUD elements and laser-dot indicators for the RPG were "overlooked", players watched the enemy. Mike Dussault (cn_083): barnacles introduced at "a safe distance" as a 'nature show'. Eric Smith (cn_084): the first physics puzzle (teeter-totter) was derived from a later complex one, "as a form of training". John Morello (cn_092): "a safe space for players to practice driving the airboat for as long as they wanted". Ted Backman (cn_024): the gravity gun was moved earlier after playtests. John Cook (cn_046): "Training under pressure is always tricky".
  - Fact-check tension: Kirchmer's cn_018 says in-world character scenes worked "much better than relying on passive storytelling or even some sort of reminder you'd find baked into a pause menu". This does not cancel XAG 109 [18] (an accessibility floor), but it ranks them: the primary objective reminder should be a short in-world WOLF or station beat, with the pause-screen objective as the always-available fallback, not the main teaching tool.
- [ONBOARDING-11] Episode One commentary (2006). Matt T. Wood ("alyxnag"): Alyx nagged ('Hurry up!', 'Keep moving!'); "after about three minutes of this, the one thing it definitely did was make the player hate Alyx"; Valve switched her "from generally leading players to almost always following"; players "especially disliked virtually any hint of bossiness". Matt T. Wood ("rollertraining"): "Training is one of the fundamental tenets of our design philosophy"; new mechanics come first "in a relatively calm but ideally still entertaining way". Scott Dalton ("3stepapproach"): "The first bridge shows players the solution, the second one confirms they understand, the third ... challenges them in a new way". Matt T. Wood ("hackingrollers"): step two of a training arc is usually "a threatening training area" in "a gated space" so the player cannot miss the point. Jake Nicholson ("powerboxes"): waiting for Alyx to finish a task left testers "more annoyed than entertained"; "whenever Alyx commits to some action in a dynamic environment, she needs to finish it quickly and move on." Jason Deakins ("holetraining"): when training happens under duress, "Alyx states the problem and helps in the fight without giving away the solution." Chet Faliszek ("alyxthekey"): "almost all of Alyx's hint lines were cut" because it was more satisfying when she "stated a goal and then stepped aside"; but players "did want to be able to explicitly request help", so hints returned "only accessible at the player's request". [Second fact-check caveat: the same node says Valve learned this "late in the development cycle and didn't have time to properly train players for this feature", so the on-request hint was never properly tested in Episode One; it is a direction, not a validated result.] Ken Birdwell ("silhouette"): attention upward needs framing, silhouette against sky and "a distinctive sound". Gautam Babbar ("bangingzombies"): a safe glimpse of a later threat behind a door. [Added in fact-check] Bill Van Buren ("alyxcitadeltone"): urgency acted through Alyx was "unsustainable"; the best approach was for her to be "always content to proceed at whatever pace the player chooses", after which impatient lines "quickly stood out and were cut". Bill Van Buren ("dog_dropship"): to deliver critical dialog Valve "temporarily block[s] the player's path using what we call a 'gate'", an obstacle only a character or event can remove.
  - Numbers: ~3 minutes of companion nagging to make players hate her; 3 steps per element (show, confirm, twist).
- [ONBOARDING-12] Episode Two commentary (2007). Gautam Babbar (EP2-008): the opening wreck lets players "reacquaint themselves with the basic movement controls in a safe, contained environment"; the earliest design had precarious jumps and "most playtesters fall to their deaths numerous times in the first five minutes", so Valve "optimized for perceived rather than actual danger." Erik Johnson (EP2-002): a goal transmission was added because testers "weren't sure where they were going, or why"; set the goal "right away". Carl Uhlman (EP2-028): a staged grub behind a web sends the nugget rolling so players learn by chasing it. Jennifer Seeley (EP2-125): a zombie pinned by a sawblade taught sawblades; "they tend to find and use sawblades without further prompting." Mike Blasczak (EP2-129): a safe landing beside toxic waste so players "slowly come to understand the toxic threat". Matt Scott (EP2-079): "If the player is laughing as he dies, it's a good sign"; head-on-keyboard means work left. Greg Coomer (EP2-074): one tester turned the wrong way "for half an hour" (mazes cut). Alfred Reynolds (EP2-119): new players read Alyx's friendly sniper beam as a threat, so her help was re-shown "make a show out of her shooting a zombie to protect the player". Marc Scaparro (EP2-155): a forced window exit taught window use. [Added in fact-check] Marc Laidlaw (EP2-003): while restoring power, Alyx "is careful not to steal the player's fun by doing it all herself".
  - Numbers: Valve does not state "0 deaths"; it saw "most playtesters fall to their deaths numerous times in the first five minutes" and redesigned for "perceived rather than actual danger". "0 lethal failures in the first ~5 min" is our derived rule. One tester repeated a wrong turn "for half an hour".
  - WOLF//OVERRIDE: WOLF must follow, not lead, in the first minutes, never nag, state a goal at most once and step aside; any WOLF help must finish quickly; hints on request (a "ask WOLF" press) rather than unsolicited; WOLF's first help should be staged as clearly friendly (the engineer must not read his first act as a threat).

### Evidence from studies and platform rules
- [ONBOARDING-13] Erik Andersen, Eleanor O'Rourke, Yun-En Liu, Richard Snider, Jeff Lowdermilk, David Truong, Seth Cooper, Zoran Popović, "The Impact of Tutorials on Games of Varying Complexity", CHI 2012 (University of Washington Center for Game Science). A/B study of "over 45,000 players" in 3 games (Refraction, Hello Worlds - a puzzle-platformer, Foldit). Tutorials raised play time "by as much as 29%" and progress "by as much as 75%" only in the most complex game (Foldit); "a surprisingly negligible effect" in the two simpler, genre-conventional games. Just-in-time instructions (vs an up-front manual) raised play time 16% and progress 40% in Foldit only. "No evidence" that restricting freedom to focus attention helps; on-demand help "improved player engagement in Foldit, but had no effect in Hello Worlds and even negative effects in Refraction" (the researcher's draft left out the Foldit gain). [Second fact-check: the results section gives the sizes: with a help button Foldit players played "about 12% longer"; in Hello Worlds players "returned about 3% less frequently"; in Refraction players with on-demand help "completed 12% fewer levels".] Players "seem to learn more from experimentation than from reading text." Caveat: these are three free browser games measured by play time and progress, not narrative games; the result transfers as "text for conventional verbs buys little", not as an exact effect size.
  - Fact-check implication for a future "ask WOLF" hint: the only on-demand-help data here is mixed (helped the complex game, did nothing in the puzzle-platformer, hurt the puzzle game). Treat an "ask WOLF" hint as a hypothesis to test, not a proven gain.
  - WOLF//OVERRIDE: walking, USE and hold-USE are conventional; a text tutorial for them buys little. Spend teaching on what is unusual: WOLF's independent choice and refusal, hold-to-crank, and timing the tank drop. Keep any instruction just-in-time, at the station.
- [ONBOARDING-14] Gifford Cheung, Thomas Zimmermann, Nachiappan Nagappan (Microsoft Research), "The First Hour Experience: How the Initial Play can Engage (or Lose) New Players", CHI PLAY 2014. From "over 200 game reviews and interviews with industry professionals": "Games are a voluntary activity and easy to abandon"; introduces "holdouts" (what keeps players despite poor design) and argues "intrigue and information should be seen as equally valuable" as fun in the first hour.
  - WOLF//OVERRIDE: the opening's job is intrigue (WOLF wakes himself, refuses the Director) plus information (who the engineer is, why they help him), not only action.
- [ONBOARDING-15] Apple, Human Interface Guidelines, "Onboarding" (change log June 10, 2024): "Ideally, people can understand your app or game simply by experiencing it"; "Teach through interactivity"; prefer "context-specific tips instead of a single onboarding flow"; "display these instructions near that area"; if you teach too much "people can feel overwhelmed"; a skipped tutorial should not be presented again on later launches but stay findable; splash screen only "long enough ... at a glance".
- [ONBOARDING-16] Apple, HIG "Designing for games" (change log June 9, 2025): "Let people play as soon as installation completes" (initial download 30 minutes or less); "Provide great default settings"; "Teach through play ... helps them feel successful right away"; a written tutorial as a reference, not "a prerequisite for gameplay". Minimum text: iOS 17 pt default / 11 pt minimum; macOS 13 / 10 pt. Buttons: iOS 44x44 pt default, 28x28 pt minimum.
- [ONBOARDING-17] Apple, HIG "Game controls" (change log June 9, 2025): touch controls "frequently used ... minimum size of 44x44 pt", menus 28x28 pt; "Always include visible and tactile press states"; avoid "controller-based naming like A, X, or R1 as artwork"; "Show and hide virtual controls to reflect gameplay" (hide when an action isn't available); combine actions into one control (touch-and-hold for a variation); with multiple controllers "use labels and glyphs that match the one that the player is actively using".
  - WOLF//OVERRIDE: the touch USE button label should be an action word or icon ("USE", a hand), not "E"; prompts already switch per device (main.gd replaces "E:"), which matches Apple; hold-to-crank fits "touch and hold" variation; hide the USE button when nothing is in range.
- [ONBOARDING-18] Microsoft, Xbox Accessibility Guideline 109 "Objective clarity" (ms.date 2023-06-08): players should "always know what goals or objectives they're supposed to be working toward"; "review tasks and objectives at any given time"; "Provide options to enable waypoint or path markers, hints, or other reminders ... when it appears that they've made no progress after a period of time (this varies by game/genre)"; tutorials must be interactive; "Static screens that display game controls ... are not sufficient"; tutorials "available on-demand ... at any point".
- [ONBOARDING-19] Game Accessibility Guidelines (collaborative, studios/specialists/academics; gameaccessibilityguidelines.com): Basic: "Include interactive tutorials", "Allow players to progress through text prompts at their own pace", start the game without many menus. Intermediate: "Indicate / allow reminder of controls during gameplay", "... of current objectives", "Include contextual in-game help / guidance / tips", "Include a means of practicing without failure", "Provide an autosave feature". Advanced: "Allow all narrative and instructions to be replayed".
  - WOLF//OVERRIDE: the pause menu should show the current objective and the controls; the opening and WOLF's key lines should be re-readable (a short log), since the top cards fade.
- [ONBOARDING-20] Celia Hodent (Epic Games), "The Gamer's Brain, Part 2: UX of Onboarding and Player Engagement", GDC 2016 (GDC Vault page, description only seen): "Engaging your audience within the first minutes of play is a delicate endeavor". Talk content not watched: details UNVERIFIED.
- [ONBOARDING-21] Marc Ballart Germà (Ubisoft Barcelona), "Teaching Complex Games: Onboarding Redesign for 'Rainbow Six Siege'", GDC 2024 slides: teach "Step by step to limit cognitive load"; loop = game explains concept and highlights input -> player presses -> game confirms and moves on; results "task completion 99%", "drop between T1 and T2 of 5%", retention "D1 +10% | D7 +5% | D28 +4%" year on year. (Multiplayer shooter; only the method transfers.)

### More Nintendo: failure-triggered help, no mean placement
- [ONBOARDING-22] Iwata Asks, Super Mario 3D Land (Iwata with Hayashida and team member Tsujimura, 2011), part 8 "A Game Anyone Can Play": Hayashida: "Assist Blocks show up after five misses" (Invincibility Leaf, P-Wing); Game Over opens a hint room; Iwata: "when people run into someplace they can't clear, they have a way to move forward."   - Numbers: help after 5 failures (3D Land); after 8 in NSMB Wii and 5 in NSMB U per MarioWiki [ONBOARDING-23].
- [ONBOARDING-23] MarioWiki, "Super Guide" (fan wiki, secondary, accessed 2026-10-12): NSMB Wii Super Guide after failing a level "at least eight times"; NSMB U after "five times"; Donkey Kong Country Returns after "eight lives". Secondary source: treat the exact counts as fan-documented.
  - WOLF//OVERRIDE: after N deaths at the same hazard (suggest 3, since the slice is short and a death costs ~20 s), WOLF offers one short, optional hint line; never before the first attempt (Miyamoto [ONBOARDING-2]).

### Portal 2 (Valve, 2011) and a few more practitioner sources
- [ONBOARDING-29] Valve, Portal 2 developer commentary (in-game, 2011; Portal Wiki transcript). ### Added in second fact-check: the closest genre match (2D side-view, no text) and tutorial funnels
- [ONBOARDING-41] Brandon Sheffield, "GDC Europe: Limbo's Carlsen On Making Players Your Worst Enemy And Your Best Friend", Game Developer (Gamasutra), 2010-08-16 (report of Jeppe Carlsen's GDC Europe 2010 talk; Carlsen was Limbo's level designer at Playdead). Director's rules: directional control and two buttons (jump and grab), and the game "should never have any tutorial text". A ledge-plus-boat scene failed because "players had no idea what to do" (nobody grabbed the boat they had just ridden), so Playdead made a new scene that forced the player near a pullable object and "put the character's arms out when he was nearby": "They stick you there until you learn" (reporter's paraphrase). Carlsen: "You could call it a learning-by-dying game"; "You don't ever really get penalized for dying". Design rules: present an element so players "most likely push [it] by running past"; "It's very important that the correct solution is fairly easy to execute", because "It takes the player a long time to go back to an idea that he had previously discarded as not being possible"; "the wrong approaches should clearly present that they are wrong"; a trap was moved off the spider's screen because players focused on the trap, not the spider; cues were placed in sound ("you can hear when the trap falls"). There is no hint system: "That's not really a fortunate situation to put the player in".
  - Numbers: 2 buttons + direction; 0 tutorial text; 9-person team; 300,000+ copies at the time.
  - WOLF//OVERRIDE: this is the nearest genre match (2D side view, small verb set, no text). For WOLF, keep the rule (the corridor is a teaching room and the tone is not Limbo's), but state its reason correctly.

## What this means for WOLF//OVERRIDE (synthesis)

## Agreement / disagreement with design-reference-25-games.md
- AGREE and strengthen: "Teach and steer by placement; show the goal first" (sec. 3 principle 5). Now backed by primary sources with numbers: Nintendo's one-at-a-time and "use one element three times" [3], Valve's 3-step arc [11] and gating [6].
- AGREE, now with the primary source: the study's summary-level line "Alyx moved from leading to following; nag lines turned players against her" (sec. 11). Episode One commentary [11] confirms it and adds the number (~3 minutes of nagging) and the fix (hints only on request).
- AGREE: "Persistent HUD 0; prompts only in USE range" (sec. 6 targets). Valve's HL2 commentary [10] (Kirchmer) supports it further: in-world character beats worked better than a pause-menu reminder. QUALIFY: XAG 109 [18] and the Game Accessibility Guidelines [19] say objectives and controls must be reviewable at any time; zero HUD should mean "nothing persistent on screen", not "nothing available". Add the objective and controls to the pause screen.
- AGREE: "Fresh-tester readability play-tests" (sec. 2 next steps). Valve [7][8][9] adds two cheap checks: ask testers to tell the story back; treat the 3rd-4th identical failure as a design bug.
- QUALIFY: the study's room pacing target "≤3.7 s of walking without a beat" (sec. 3) is derived from Lana criticism; onboarding sources do not give a per-view number either. GMTK [31] gives ~5 minutes per taught idea in Mario, Portal [7] ~60 s in the first room. Keep the study's target as a hypothesis to validate by play.
- EXTEND (fills a listed gap): "Touch target sizes" were "not supplied" (sec. 0 and sec. 9). Apple HIG [16][17]: frequently used touch controls >= 44x44 pt, secondary >= 28x28 pt, visible press states, action symbols not "A/X/R1" labels.
- MILD DISAGREEMENT (fact-check: this decision is in docs/research-2026-10/README.md and codex-design-review.md, not in design-reference-25-games.md; on-demand help data in [13] is mixed, so treat this as a hypothesis): the decision note "WOLF: ... no player request verb yet". The strongest onboarding evidence for companion hints [11] (Faliszek) says players disliked unsolicited hints but wanted to request help. This argues for a future "ask WOLF" request (an ask he can decline), at least for hints; it does not argue against today's slice, but the no-request-verb rule should be revisited once deaths cluster at one hazard.
- MILD DISAGREEMENT: the study's death target "≲20 s replay" is right for later rooms, but the onboarding evidence [12] says the first ~5 minutes should contain no deaths at all (perceived, not actual, danger). The corridor already complies; the study should state it as a rule so later edits do not add an early lethal hazard.
- AGREE: "Height changes must never ask for precision" and "Fail-able jumps 0" (sec. 3). HL2 Ep2's opening rework [12] is a direct example of why.
