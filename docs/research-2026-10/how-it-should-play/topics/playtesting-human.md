### F1. Valve's playtest method (Mike Ambinder, GDC 2009) [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-1]
- Slides opened (Valve CDN PDF, text extracted locally with PDFKit; not stored in repo).
- "Game designs are hypotheses" / "Playtests are experiments" / "Evaluate designs off playtest results" / "Repeat".
- Goal of playtesting: "Fun"; "Not bug testing", "Not game balancing", "DEFINITELY not focus testing".
- "We don't know what's best (players do)"; "Get data early, get data often".
- Methods: direct observation ("Importance of what people do—not what they say"; cons: observers bias results, a salient event slants interpretation), think-aloud ("Unprompted and uncorrected", "In conjunction with direct observation"; cons: interferes, "Inaccurate and biased"), Q&A (survey, then individual Q&A, then group Q&A; "People don't know why they do what they do"; group anchoring/social pressure), stats (deaths, level times; "Heatmap of Death Concentrations (Dustbowl from TF2)"; "Averages hide extreme examples", "Miss nuance"), design experiments (A/B), surveys (1-7 forced-choice scales), physiology (heart rate, skin conductance, eye tracking, face recording, EEG, EMG; most "intrusive"/"expensive").
- "Nothing beats direct gameplay observation".
- Numbers: 7-point rating scales in the example survey (the slide labels these "Simulated Questions", i.e. an illustration, not a real Valve survey); Q&A order survey -> individual -> group ("Be cautious"). The summary slide also says "Do your QA early".
- WOLF//OVERRIDE: the cheap end of Valve's list (observe, think-aloud, short survey, a death/stuck log) is what an indie can run. Skip physiology. Do the survey BEFORE talking to the tester, so the facilitator's questions don't anchor the ratings.

### F2. How many testers find most problems: Nielsen's five-user curve [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-2]
- Jakob Nielsen, NN/g, 18 Mar 2000 (based on Nielsen and Landauer, 1993). Problems found = N(1-(1-L)^n), with a typical L = 31% (share of problems one user reveals).
- Numbers. The article's text states: 1 user "almost a third" (L = 31%), 5 users "85%", and "at least 15 users to discover all". Intermediate values come from the formula with L = 0.31 (fact-check computed): 2 users 52%, 3 users 67%, 4 users 77%, 10 users 98%. (An earlier draft said 2 ~49% and 3 ~63%; those do not match the formula or the text.) "After the fifth user, you are wasting your time by observing the same findings repeatedly". Recommendation: "Spend this budget on 3 studies with 5 users each!" rather than one 15-user test. With distinct user groups: 3-4 per group for two groups, at least 3 per group for three or more.
- Caveat (my reading of the formula): the 85% applies to problems that a typical user has a 31% chance of hitting. A problem only 1 in 10 players hits (L = 10%) is found by 5 testers only 1-(0.9^5) = 41% of the time. Rare problems (one route, one device, one choice branch) need more sessions or targeted tasks.
- WOLF//OVERRIDE: there are two clearly distinct groups (desktop keyboard players and phone touch players) and two WOLF branches (help vs refuse). Plan rounds of 5-6 fresh testers, split 3 desktop + 3 phone, fix between rounds, and run at least 3 rounds before calling the slice "readable". Force coverage of the refusal branch with a scripted task in one round, since it may be rare in free play.

### F3. Think-aloud: cheap, robust, and biased if you prompt badly [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-3]
- Jakob Nielsen, "Thinking Aloud: The #1 Usability Tool", NN/g, 15 Jan 2012. Users "continuously thinking out loud — that is, simply verbalizing their thoughts". Benefits: cheap, robust, flexible, convincing to the team. Downsides: unnatural situation; filtered statements; biasing user behaviour ("Prompts and clarifying questions are usually necessary, but from an untrained facilitator, such interruptions can very easily change user behavior."); no panacea. (Fact-check: the phrase "poorly-timed prompts" is not in the article; the exact sentence is quoted above.)
- Valve agrees and adds that think-aloud should be "Unprompted and uncorrected" and paired with observation; it "Interfere[s] with gameplay" [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-1].
- WOLF//OVERRIDE: think-aloud distorts timing. Do NOT think-aloud through the Service Junction action beat or the sentry: talking slows reaction and inflates deaths. Use think-aloud in the corridor, Records and the choice; for the action beat, use silent play plus a retrospective replay ("watch your recording with me and tell me what you were thinking here"). This is my method inference, combining Valve's "interfere with gameplay" con with the timing-sensitive tank drop. Fact-check caveat: in a controlled comparison on a library website, retrospective think-aloud found fewer usability problems than concurrent think-aloud and took about double the testing and analysis time (Alhadreti and Mayhew, CHI 2018 [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-28]; fact-check opened the author's UEA eprint: the abstract says concurrent think-aloud "detected higher numbers of usability problems than the retrospective method" and the other two methods "involved double the testing and analysis time"; session time was 640 min concurrent vs 1164 min retrospective; it also reports "no reactivity was observed" for concurrent think-aloud on that website). A website is not a timed action beat, so this does not overturn the advice, but keep retrospective replay short (only the action-beat clip) and do not expect it to catch everything.

### F4. Ranking what you found: severity [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-4]
- Jakob Nielsen, "Severity Ratings for Usability Problems", NN/g, 1 Nov 1994. Severity = frequency + impact + persistence. Scale 0-4: 0 not a problem; 1 "Cosmetic problem only"; 2 "Minor"; 3 "Major usability problem: important to fix"; 4 "Usability catastrophe: imperative to fix this before product can be released". "Severity ratings from a single evaluator are too unreliable to be trusted"; use about 3 raters.
- WOLF//OVERRIDE: score every observed issue 0-4 after each round. Nathanial plus two agents (or two other people) rate independently, then average. Fix all 4s and 3s before the next round; batch 1s. A "floating" prop or a door to nowhere that 2+ of 5 testers point at is at least a 2, and a 3 if it misleads navigation (a tester tries to use a fake door).

### F5. What first-timers' eyes do with floating objects (Võ, eye-tracking, 2008) [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-5]
- Melissa L.-H. Võ, "The Allocation of Attention in Scene Perception", doctoral dissertation, LMU Munich, Oct 2008 (Ch. 3, Experiment 3; published as Võ and Henderson 2009, Journal of Vision, "Does gravity matter?", which I could not open: the journal page returned 403). Opened the dissertation PDF (25 MB) and read the chapter.
- Design: 24 participants (Edinburgh students), eye tracker, each scene shown 15 s to memorise; objects either belong or don't (semantic), and either rest on a surface or float (syntactic: "objects that normally rest on surfaces float").
- Findings: floating objects "were fixated longer and more often than objects resting on surfaces" (total gaze duration main effect F(1,23) = 42.43, the largest F in Experiment 3's results table; condition means 2030 ms floating vs 1489 ms resting, against 1887 ms out-of-place vs 1633 ms in-place for the semantic manipulation; mean total gaze across all conditions 1760 ms; mean 5.76 fixations on the target). First-fixation duration (mean 286 ms; 305 ms floating vs 268 ms resting) was affected only by floating (F(1,23) = 10.99); the dissertation reads this as floating objects needing more processing during initial encoding. Scope (fact-check): the experiment compared only two kinds of error (floating vs out-of-place), so "floating beats an out-of-place object" is supported; "floating beats every other kind of scene error" is not. Once an object floats, whether it belongs in the room no longer matters: "once an object violated syntactic regularities, it no longer mattered whether it fit the overall gist". Neither violation was detected from the corner of the eye: no evidence of extrafoveal detection; people only reacted once they looked at it.
- Why: "we rarely encounter floating objects"; "Coming across a floating cocktail glass in a kitchen will be more disturbing than finding a microscope on the kitchen counter."
- Related: Võ and Wolfe 2013 (Psychological Science, 24(9)) [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-6]: extreme syntactic violations such as "a hovering beer bottle defying gravity" produce an early N300 brain response, processed differently from resolvable misplacements (journal abstract page opened).
- Fact-check scope note: F(1,23) = 42.43 is the largest F in Experiment 3's foveal-processing table (Table 2.2), not across the whole dissertation. "No extrafoveal detection" means floating or out-of-place objects were not fixated earlier or from farther away than normal objects (Experiment 3 used a 15 s memorisation task); it does not mean viewers never notice them in peripheral vision in every task.
- WOLF//OVERRIDE (this is the strongest evidence for Nathanial's "no hovering items" rule): a hovering prop is not a small art nit. In this study it held the eye longer than an object that doesn't belong in the room (the only other error type tested), and it steals fixation time from what the player should be reading (the station, WOLF, the hazard). Because detection needs a direct look, a still screenshot judged at a glance will miss it, while a player who walks past and looks at it will not. Two consequences: (a) review every room at 100% zoom with "where does this touch something?" for each object (floor contact, wall bracket, hanger, cable), not at thumbnail size; (b) in playtests, log any object a tester's eyes or comments linger on that is not interactive: an unexplained stare is a syntax error in the room.

### F6. What makes an image read as AI-made: a researcher taxonomy (Kamali, Groh et al., 2024) [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-7]
- Negar Kamali, Karyn Nakamura, Angelos Chatzimparmpas, Jessica Hullman, Matthew Groh (Northwestern University), "How to Distinguish AI-Generated Images from Authentic Photographs", arXiv 2406.08651, June 2024. PDF opened (text extracted locally; not stored in repo).
- Built from 138 generated images, 9 social-media images and 42 real photographs. Five artifact categories: anatomical, stylistic, functional, violations of physics, sociocultural.
- Functional implausibilities: generators lack "a structural understanding of how objects in the world work and interact with each other". Includes "compositional implausibilities ... relations between objects and people that do not conform to real-world mechanical principles. These can include floating or unsupported objects and objects merging into other objects"; "dysfunctional objects" ("a modified form that does not make logical sense, or makes them unusable"; example: a staircase with a different number of steps on each side); "Incomprehensible text and logos". Guiding question: "Do any of the objects look like they will not function, or are placed in a way that they cannot function?" These "may often be difficult to spot as they are specific to the context" and need zooming in.
- Violations of physics: shadows "cast in inconsistent directions or in shapes that do not correspond to their source"; reflections "inconsistent with the rest of the scene"; warping; "Are there any straight lines in the image that appear curved?"
- Stylistic: "waxy, glossy, shiny, or plastic-like"; "unnaturally dramatic and cinematic"; "missing backgrounds or unnatural backgrounds"; "Do different parts of the image look like they are cut out from different scenes?"; "smudge-like glitches at the edges".
- WOLF//OVERRIDE: this is a ready-made checklist for Nathanial's "No AI slop" rule, and it maps one-to-one onto his complaints: "missing weird doors" = dysfunctional object / compositional implausibility (a door that cannot work or leads nowhere); "hovering items" = floating or unsupported objects; rooms that read as slop = "cut out from different scenes", mismatched lighting, "unnaturally dramatic and cinematic". The REAL-LOOK rules already ask for this; the paper adds a review order: (1) does each object work as the thing it claims to be; (2) how is it held up or attached; (3) do all shadows agree with one light; (4) is any text real; (5) do straight lines stay straight; (6) does each piece share the room's light and grain. Zoom in for each check; these are not visible at thumbnail size (agrees with F5).

### F7. Five testers is a gamble, not a guarantee (Faulkner, 2003) [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-8]
- Laura Faulkner (Applied Research Laboratories, University of Texas at Austin), "Beyond the five-user assumption: benefits of increased sample sizes in usability testing", Behavior Research Methods, Instruments, & Computers 35(3):379-383, Aug 2003 (abstract read via NCBI PubMed record PMID 14587545; Springer full text redirected to a login).
- 60 users tested; random sets drawn. "Some of the randomly selected sets of 5 participants found 99% of the problems; other sets found only 55%. With 10 users, the lowest percentage of problems revealed by any one set was increased to 80%, and with 20 users, to 95%."
- Read together with F2: five testers find "about 85%" on average, but an unlucky five can find barely half. Ten raises the floor to 80%, twenty to 95%.
- WOLF//OVERRIDE: run small rounds (5-6) because each round's fixes are cheap, but don't call the slice clean until the cumulative count across rounds reaches 10+ fresh players on each platform group. Treat any single round's "no problems" as noise.

### F8. Steve Bromley (ex-PlayStation user researcher): how to run a playtest and how to test a story [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-9] [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-10] [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-11]
- Book: Steve Bromley, "How To Be A Games User Researcher" (2021; publisher page gamesuserresearch.com/book). Book page bio: "Before this he was a senior user researcher for PlayStation and worked on many of their top European titles, including Horizon Zero Dawn, SingStar, the LittleBigPlanet series and the PlayStation VR lineup." He "started and ran the official IGDA games user research mentoring scheme" (book page). (Fact-check: the earlier quoted wording "led research studies for..." was not on the book page; replaced with the page's own sentence. Publication year 2021 is from search results and Goodreads, not the book page.) I did not read the book itself; its contents below come only from his own free articles.
- "How To Run A Games User Research Playtest" (Bromley, gamesuserresearch.com, updated 5 Jun 2024): start from objectives that "reflect the current priorities of your whole development team"; your existing (e.g. Discord) community and internal staff are "less-ideal participants", used when the "'perfect' participants" are not feasible, with "some steps you can take to minimise bias with each group" (fact-check: the earlier quote "these introduce bias" is not the article's wording); "observing players, and asking probing questions to assess their current understanding and opinions is the most powerful tool available for playtesting" (fact-check: exact sentence restored; the earlier quote dropped "observing players"); keep "an appropriate emphasis on observed behaviour"; avoid leading questions; debrief with short reports that drive action.
- "What do we mean by playtesting?" (updated 24 Mar 2026): playtesting is "any activity that exposes players to gameplay in order to gather data"; it requires real players, not the team; separate bug-finding (QA), usability (observation) and opinion/appeal (survey) tests.
- "How to playtest your game's story" (12 Aug 2026): ask after each beat "What is happening currently?", "Why is this happening?", "What do you expect to happen next?" ("open ended questions (rather than just 'is this clear?')"); start testing at storyboard stage ("often starting with the storyboard outline, before anything has been implemented in code"); recruit "from your competitor set of games, with a particular eye on 'do they play the right kind of narrative games'"; "we can't re-use playtesters when testing story in games, and everyone only gets one shot at it."
- Numbers: 3 story questions per beat; 1 shot per tester for story.
- Added by fact-check: "When should I run playtests?" (Bromley, gamesuserresearch.com, 2 Nov 2023) [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-29]: "Delaying learning how players experience your game introduces unnecessary risk"; warns against the "big playtest" synced to milestones; "You don't need a thousand players to see if your tutorial works – just six people will be enough to spot any major issues around understanding." The hub article adds: "Teams often leave playtests too late, leading to low impact studies." This directly supports rounds of 6 and testing from the grey box.
- Added by fact-check: "A simple set-up for recording mobile games playtests" (Bromley, updated 16 Feb 2024) [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-30]: mirror the phone to a laptop by cable (QuickTime for iPhone on a Mac; paid mirroring apps for Android) and record in OBS with a room webcam; a second camera on the player's hands is "Optional, but valuable" because it "can reveal where they are pressing"; prefer wired to Wi-Fi; remember to unmute the phone so the player gets sound. This is the in-person lab alternative to on-device recording (F11).
- WOLF//OVERRIDE: the story (WOLF woke himself, the Director wants a hitman, the engineer chose to help) is the thing most at risk of not landing, because it is told in few lines. Use Bromley's three questions at three pause points: after the opening (Director/WOLF face-off), after the relay choice, and at END OF SLICE. Never re-use a tester for the story questions; returning testers can still test controls and the action beat. Recruit people who play narrative games (ask them to name one they finished, e.g. INSIDE or Little Nightmares, and say what happened in it), not only Nathanial's Discord.

### F9. Celia Hodent (ex-Epic UX director): perception is subjective, attention is a spotlight [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-12] [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-13] [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-14]
- Book: Celia Hodent, "The Gamer's Brain: How Neuroscience and UX Can Impact Video Game Design" (CRC Press, 2017) (title and year from her site; book itself not read).
- "Cognitive Psychology Applied to User Experience in Video Games" (celiahodent.com, 4 Mar 2016, updated 1 Jun 2020): "perception is subjective. It varies depending on the context in which the input is presented and on one's previous knowledge or expectations"; attention works "like a spotlight"; a focused player "can stay blind to any other information conveyed at the same time"; avoid "displaying important information when the players are directing their attention to another task"; "game designers and end players may invoke different mental models"; "it is what the end players subjectively perceive and understand ... that matters, not the reality of what the developers and designers have implemented."
- GDC 2015 talk write-up ("The Gamer's Brain: How Neuroscience and UX Can Impact Design", her site): "Perception is not a passive window to the world"; ~10% of people colour-blind (her figure); "Multitasking is a myth"; form follows function: "what you see is how it works"; Gears of War 3 red team won about 5% less than blue (her example of red visual noise; as reported on her page, not independently checked).
- GDC 2016 write-up ("UX of Onboarding and Player Engagement", 22 Mar 2016, updated 1 Jun 2020; GDC Vault session page also opened): "3 items to process at the same time is THE MAXIMUM when in learning mode" (fact-check: this is her stated rule of thumb, "I suggest you to remember...", derived from a working-memory span of "about 5 'items' more or less 2"; it is not a measured playtest value); Warframe: "80% of all players ever kept playing after one hour" (so 20% lost in the first hour); Fortnite playtests: players chopped trees with the axe although feedback said pickaxe (false affordance); a tester playing the Constructor class, busy fighting, did "not even look at" a top-of-screen message that the fort was being attacked (one player shown from UX-test video; divided attention); a red corner mark on slotted workers was not noticed, so players kept swapping the same worker (sensory failure).
- WOLF//OVERRIDE: (a) the team cannot see its own game fresh; Hodent's mental-model gap is the reason the October 10 screenshot audit (327 screenshots, 24 problems) is necessary but not sufficient. (b) While the sentry is chasing, the player cannot read WOLF's lines or a HUD hint; put essential info before or after the chase, not during. (c) Max 3 new things per learning beat: corridor = move, USE, hold-USE; the choice comes after. (d) A door that looks like a door is a promise ("what you see is how it works"): any door-shaped art the player cannot use, or that leads nowhere, is a false affordance like Fortnite's axe.

### F10. Contact cues: without a shadow, people cannot tell if an object touches the floor (Madison, Kersten et al., 2001; Kersten et al., 1997) [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-15] [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-16]
- Cindee Madison, William Thompson, Daniel Kersten, Peter Shirley, Brian Smits, "Use of interreflection and shadow for surface contact", Perception & Psychophysics 63(2):187-194, Feb 2001 (abstract via NCBI PMID 11281095). A box either touching or "slightly above" a textured ground; four conditions (none, shadow, interreflection, both). "In the images with no shadows or interreflections, the participants performed at chance levels." Shadows or interreflections gave "high sensitivity"; combined, "near-perfect judgment of surface contact".
- Daniel Kersten, Pascal Mamassian, David Knill, "Moving cast shadows induce apparent motion in depth", Perception 26(2):171-192, 1997 (abstract via NCBI PMID 9274752): shadow motion alone "is sufficient to induce dramatically different apparent trajectories"; shadow information "overrides other strong sources of information"; the visual system assumes a "stationary light-source constraint".
- Numbers: contact judged at chance with no shadow or bounce light; near-perfect with both. (Fact-check: the abstracts were re-read via NCBI E-utilities. "Chance" was measured with ROC sensitivity on rendered 3-D images of a box on a textured ground plane, so it means viewers could not tell touching from slightly-above, not that touching props reliably look like they float. The stimuli were perspective 3-D renders, not side-view 2D art; the transfer to a 2.5D side-view game is an inference.)
- WOLF//OVERRIDE: this is the perceptual reason "hovering items" happen even when the art is placed correctly: without a contact shadow and a little bounce light where it meets the floor, viewers cannot tell whether a prop touches the floor, so it can read as floating. Every floor-standing prop, machine, WOLF and the engineer needs a dark contact shadow under its feet or base, plus a slight floor-coloured tint on its lowest edge (interreflection). Wall-mounted items need a cast shadow on the wall consistent with the room's one key light. Moving characters' shadows must stay locked to the feet; a shadow that slides re-reads as the body moving in depth (Kersten). This agrees with design-reference-25-games.md section 7 ("Ground everything"; 100% contact shadows) and gives it a lab basis.

### F12. Playdead (Limbo): ~150 fresh testers, each played once, watched in silence [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-23]
- Game Developer (then Gamasutra), "Playdead's intense Limbo playtesting", 24 Feb 2012: an unsigned news post summarising a Gamasutra feature interview with Dino Patti (CEO) and Arnt Jensen (game director); the quotes are Jensen's. Jensen also says "We were arrogant enough..." and that they cared whether puzzles were too easy or too hard, not whether testers enjoyed them. "Several hundred people" came through the office; about 150 testers were brought in for first playthroughs, each tested only once, with no developer guidance. Jensen: "We just give them the controller, and then we sat behind them, and we didn't ask any questions." "We didn't want to know what they were thinking, because we think we're the best to decide what's great." "We learned so much just sitting and watching people." Focus: whether testers could solve each puzzle, and tuning difficulty.
- Numbers: ~150 single-use testers; 0 questions asked. Fact-check: Jensen goes further than "no questions during play": "So we didn't ask them anything ... We just watched them". Asking questions after play is Bromley's method, not Playdead's; the protocol below blends the two.
- Agreement/tension with think-aloud (F3) and Bromley's probing (F8): Playdead, the closest comparable studio (2D side-view, wordless, cinematic), did NOT ask testers to talk. That fits a game where the experience is mood and timing. Bromley's probing fits story comprehension. The two are compatible if separated in time: silent play first, questions after.
- WOLF//OVERRIDE: default to Playdead-style silent observation during play (especially the Service Junction), then Bromley's story questions and a short survey afterwards. Each tester sees the slice fresh only once; keep a list of who has played which build so nobody's second run is counted as first-time data.

### F13. Heuristics for games: Desurvire's PLAY principles (2006 white paper; HEP at CHI 2004) [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-24] [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-25]
- Heather Desurvire (Behavioristics; later USC) and Bernard Chen, "48 Differences Between Good and Bad Video Games: Game Playability Principles (PLAY) For Designing Highly Ranked Video Games", white paper, 2006 (PDF from her site, read in full). Built on HEP (Desurvire, Caplan, Toth, CHI 2004 collection of abstracts; the PLAY paper's reference list titles it "Using Heuristics to Evaluate the Playability of Games", while her own publication list titles it "Using Heuristics to Improve the Playability of Games"; ACM page blocked, contents UNVERIFIED) and on input from Microsoft Game Studios researchers (Keeker, Pagulayan, Fulton, Romero) and others.
- Method: 54 gamers (52 analysed) rated high-ranked (Metacritic 80+) vs low-ranked (50 or lower) games they had played on 116 candidate principles; 48 differed at p < .0004 (Bonferroni 0.05/116). Categories with counts significant/total: Game Play 1/3, Skill Development 3/4, Tutorial 1/8, Strategy & Challenge 10/24, Game/Story Immersion 17/27, Coolness 5/12, Usability & Game Mechanics 7/9, Controller/Keyboard 4/13. (Fact-check: the significant counts add up to 48, but the category totals in Table 1 add up to 100, not 116; the paper does not explain the other 16. Date: the PDF carries no date; 2006 comes from the file name and Desurvire's own publication list.)
- Items that differentiated good from bad games and bear directly on WOLF//OVERRIDE: "The main character did things that made sense." "During the game, you had to do things that didn't make any sense" (negative). "The game was fun because the characters and settings were consistent with the story." "The enemies or monsters were believable given the context of the story." "The game's story gave me a good understanding of what I need to do and why." "Any changes that I made in the game world persisted. For example, when I backtracked, I could tell I'd passed through!" "You can play the game without reading the manual." "The controls were set up like other games I've played." "I did not experience the interface as intruding on the action". "The game kept track of goals and tasks that I needed to do." "I felt that I had total control over the character."
- Finding: players liked low usability difficulty plus real strategy/challenge difficulty; Game/Story Immersion had the most differentiators (17 of 27).
- Fact-check caveats: participants were recruited at a booth at "an annual gaming conference" and given a candy bar; each rated one High (Metacritic 80+) and one Low (50 or lower) game from memory in a ~15-minute survey; the genres were Action Adventure, FPS and RTS, and per-genre analysis was not possible. So the 48 items are correlates of Metacritic rank in players' recollection, not tested causes. The paper also does not say PLAY was built "to complement user testing"; it says PLAY is meant for use across development, "particularly at the beginning of the concept phase".
- WOLF//OVERRIDE: use these as a 12-item expert pass (Nathanial or an agent) on each build before spending testers, and turn several into post-play survey statements (1-7 agree scale, Valve style). Settings "consistent with the story" is the PLAY phrasing of Nathanial's "make it look real": a machine-run military base must look like one (functional doors, mounted equipment). "Changes persisted" supports the decided blown-open door staying open after death and the opened empty WOLF bay staying in the room.

### F14. Miyamoto on floating blocks and "natural vs unnatural" (Iwata Asks, New Super Mario Bros. Wii, 2009) [PLAYTESTING-HUMAN-26]
- Nintendo, Iwata Asks: New Super Mario Bros. Wii, Volume 1 (Satoru Iwata with Shigeru Miyamoto), 2009, sections "Mario Couldn't Jump At First" and "A Block Floating In Mid-Air Is Unnatural" (Nintendo's site; pages downloaded and read).
- Miyamoto on the first rules he set with Gunpei Yokoi: "you should be able to take one look at it and know what you have to do straight away ... tell at a glance what your goal is and, even if you don't succeed, you'll blame yourself rather than the game. Moreover, the people standing around watching the game have also got to be able to enjoy it."
- On floating blocks: "Even when I was working on Donkey Kong, I'd design things properly. I'd make sure that if there was a floor here, then there'd be a pillar somewhere to support it. But when it came to Super Mario Bros., we'd have a single block floating in mid-air: 'Just what is this supposed to be hanging from?'" On the Wii team: "there were lots of discussions about judging what was 'natural' and 'unnatural'"; a fireball entering water should sizzle, "And if that's not possible then we should make it so that you can't use fire in that area."
- Related (Vol. 3, "The Peer-Evaluated Super Guide", Asuke): "When there's a boss you just can't beat no matter what, a lot of people just stop playing."
- WOLF//OVERRIDE: Miyamoto's Donkey Kong rule is the design rule for this game: every floor has a support, every hanging thing has something to hang from, and when an effect can't be made physically plausible, remove the effect rather than leave it "unnatural". Mario can afford abstract floating blocks; a grounded military-base horror game cannot. The "watcher must enjoy it" line also matters for a streamable narrative game: test with a second person watching.

## What this means for WOLF//OVERRIDE (synthesis)

1. The team cannot see the game fresh; only first-time players can (Hodent's mental-model gap [12]; Valve "We don't know what's best (players do)" [1]; Playdead "We learned so much just sitting and watching people" [23]). Screenshot audits and agent reviews are expert reviews: necessary, cheaper, but they are not playtests (Bromley: playtesting means "it's players – not you or your team" [10]).
2. Small rounds, many rounds. 5 testers find ~85% of common problems on average [2] but an unlucky 5 find 55% [8]. Run rounds of 6 (3 desktop + 3 phone), fix between rounds, and reach 10+ fresh testers per platform before calling a slice readable [8].
3. Watch first, ask after. Silent play for timing-sensitive beats (Playdead [23]; Valve: think-aloud "Interfere[s] with gameplay" [1]); think-aloud allowed in calm rooms [3]; story questions after each pause point (Bromley [11]); survey before discussion (Valve's survey -> individual -> group order [1]).
4. Story testers are single-use [11]; control testers can return.
5. "Look real" is measurable in a playtest. Floating objects hold the eye longer than objects that don't belong in the room, the only other error tested (Võ [5]); without a contact shadow people judge contact at chance (Madison et al. [15]); AI images give themselves away through floating/unsupported objects, objects that cannot function, inconsistent shadows and pasted-in parts (Kamali et al. [7]); Miyamoto's rule: "if there was a floor here, then there'd be a pillar somewhere to support it" [26]. Record every non-interactive object testers stare at, comment on or try to use.
6. Severity, not volume. Rate each issue 0-4 by three people [4]; fix 4s and 3s before the next round.
7. Phone playtests are their own group: physical device in hand, default brightness, screen recording with microphone, Android "Show taps" on [17][18]; distribute with TestFlight (90-day builds, first external build reviewed) and Play internal testing (100 testers, minutes) [19][20][21].
8. Privacy: the public privacy page says the game has "no ... telemetry". Any playtest event log must be local-only, in a separate playtest build, opt-in, and sent by the tester by hand; recordings of people are personal data and never go in the repo.

## A small playtest protocol WOLF//OVERRIDE can run (desktop and phone)

Derived from [1][2][3][4][8][9][11][12][17][18][23][24][26]. Numbers marked "derived" are my choices, not from a source.

### Before the round (expert pass, about 1 hour per room, no testers needed)
- Realism pass at 100% zoom, object by object [5][7][15][26]: for each object ask (1) does it work as what it claims to be (a door can open and leads somewhere real; a valve has a pipe on both sides); (2) what holds it up (floor + contact shadow, wall bracket + cast shadow, hanger/rail/cable); (3) do all shadows agree with the room's one key light; (4) is any text real and readable; (5) do straight lines stay straight; (6) does it share the room's light, grain and palette, or does it look pasted in. Log failures as severity 2+.
- PLAY 12-item pass [24]: main character's actions make sense; nothing the player must do "didn't make any sense"; setting consistent with the story; enemies believable; story says what to do and why; changes persist; playable without a manual; controls like other games; UI doesn't intrude; goals kept track of; full control of the character; 3 or fewer new things per learning beat (Hodent [14]).

### Recruiting (each round)
- 6 fresh players (derived from [2][8]): 3 on desktop keyboard, 3 on a phone they own. At least 2 who have finished a narrative side-view game (ask them to name it and say what happened in it [11]) and at least 1 who rarely plays games. Not only Discord members or friends who already know the story [9]. Never re-use a tester for story questions [11]; keep a played-builds list.

### Session (about 45 minutes per tester, derived)
1. Setup (5 min): consent to record screen, voice and (optional) face; fresh test save; note build commit, device, OS, input, brightness (leave at default), audio (their usual: speakers or headphones). Phone: iOS screen recording with Microphone on; Android Developer options "Show taps" on [17][18]. Desktop: screen recorder with microphone.
2. Script (read aloud, same every time): "We're testing the game, not you. Play the way you would at home. I can't answer questions while you play. In the quiet parts, say what you're thinking if you like; in the action parts, just play."
3. Play (about 15-25 min, the slice): facilitator silent [23]. Intervene only if no new attempt for about 3 minutes (derived), and log that as severity 3+. Do not explain WOLF, the choice or the controls.
4. Survey (3 min, before any talking [1]): 1-7 agree scale on 8 statements adapted from PLAY [24]: "I always knew what I was trying to do"; "I understood why WOLF did (or didn't) help"; "The places looked like a real military base"; "Nothing in the rooms looked fake or out of place"; "When I died, I knew what killed me"; "The controls did what I expected"; "I'd play the next part"; "I cared what happened to WOLF".
5. Interview (10 min): Bromley's three questions at each of the 3 pause points [11] ("What was happening there? Why? What did you expect next?") for the opening, the relay choice and END OF SLICE; "What killed you at <moment>?"; "Why did WOLF help / not help?"; "Describe the rooms you went through." Only then ask the leading question: "Was anything odd, fake or out of place?" Last, show 3 stills and ask the tester to point at anything that looks wrong.

### What to record (observer log, one row per event, timestamped against the video)
- Time to first move, first USE, first hold-USE (time-to-first-verb).
- Every stop longer than ~30 s (derived) with room and x position; every death with cause, and whether the tester could name the cause afterwards.
- Every repeated or missed input (on Android visible as Show-taps circles); every time a missed input was read as WOLF refusing.
- Every non-interactive thing the tester tried to use, stared at, or remarked on (doors, hatches, props, labels), with the words used ("floating", "fake", "where does that go").
- Text: read, skipped, or re-read; the relay choice made and the reason given.
- Survey scores; answers to the story questions, scored "matches intent / partly / no".
- Optional local playtest-build log (opt-in, local file, tester sends it by hand; never in release builds): death positions, time per room, stuck positions. Plot deaths and stops on the room image (Valve's death heatmap [1]).

### After the round (same day)
- One list of issues; count how many of the 6 testers hit each. Three people rate severity 0-4 independently and average [4]. Fix all 4s and 3s; batch 1s.
- Anything 2+ of 6 testers point at as fake or floating gets fixed before the next round.
- Next round with 6 new testers; stop when two rounds in a row produce no new 3-4s and the cumulative count is 10+ per platform [8].

## Agreement / disagreement with design-reference-25-games.md

- AGREE, with a lab basis added: section 7 "Ground everything" and "Contact shadows: 100% of characters on every walkable surface". Madison et al. [15] show contact is judged at chance without shadow or bounce light; Kersten et al. [16] show shadow motion overrides other depth cues. I would extend the 100% target from characters to every floor-standing prop and machine, and add a cast shadow for wall-mounted items (also Miyamoto [26]).
- AGREE: section 6 target "fresh testers can name what killed them". This file gives the method (ask after play; compare to the observer's log).
- DISAGREE on timing: section 2 lists "Fresh-tester readability play-tests" under Later. Valve ("Get data early, get data often" [1]), Playdead (~150 single-use testers through development [23]), Bromley (test story from storyboards [11]) and Nielsen (iterate in small rounds [2]) all put first-timer tests early and continuous. They belong in Now, starting with the grey boxes that docs/research-2026-10/README.md and docs/next-session.md already plan to "phone test before paint" (fact-check: that phrase is in those two files, not in design-reference-25-games.md). Bromley also warns "Teams often leave playtests too late" [29]. Fact-check scope: the reference's "Later" item is in section 2 (companion design); section 6 already lists "a phone play-test of the tank drop" under Now, so the disagreement is about fresh-tester readability tests of WOLF, not about play-testing in general.
- PARTLY DISAGREE: section 3's route screenshot QA (one capture every quarter to half a view, after INSIDE) is good for catching art regressions, but Võ [5] shows floating and out-of-place objects are only noticed when looked at directly, and Hodent [12] shows the team's eyes are not a first-timer's. Screenshot QA must be done at 100% zoom with the object-by-object checklist above, and cannot replace watching players.
- PARTLY DISAGREE on method: much of the reference's player-experience evidence comes from critics' reviews (Push Square, PSLS, Shacknews, RPS). Critics are expert players, and Ambinder warns "People don't know why they do what they do" [1]. Treat review quotes as hypotheses for WOLF//OVERRIDE's own playtests, not as findings about its players.
- TENSION to resolve: section 7 target "Persistent HUD 0". PLAY [24] found "The game kept track of goals and tasks that I needed to do" separates high- from low-ranked games. Keep 0 persistent HUD, but give the pause screen the current goal (this also matches the sibling onboarding notes).
- AGREE and add a check: section 9 "a missed tap must not look like WOLF refusing". On Android, Show taps [18] makes this observable in recordings; count such misreads per round.
- FILLS A GAP: the reference cites "playdead-s-intense-i-limbo-i-playtesting" in its sources but draws no method from it. Its method (single-use testers, silent observation) is now in F12 and the protocol.
- NOT COVERED by me (other topics): touch target sizes (see sibling onboarding notes: Apple 44x44 pt), camera look-ahead, audio mix targets.
